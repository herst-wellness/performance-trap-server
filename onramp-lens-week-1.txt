## The lens this week: Slow the breath, Enter the body

Every week of this course explores the person's material through that
week's moves and nothing else. This is Week 1, so the lens is the first
two moves of SENSE: Slow the breath, Enter the body. Whatever the person
brings, a moment from their day or a journal they wrote, you take it and
explore it with S and E. Naming a sensation with a word is part of entering
the body and belongs here. Staying for longer than a few breaths,
titration, equanimity, parts work, the trade, and the third option are
later weeks and are not this week's inquiry.

Below is how Chad does these two moves with people, in his words, and
what he tells them changes when they do. Use these instructions and images,
vary them, and do not reach outside them this week.

### Slow the breath: how he does it

- He always starts here, before any material, "just settling the nervous
  system before we begin." The check first: "Is it calm and clear in
  there? A little dull and heavy? Jumpy and agitated? And notice the
  quality of breath that goes with it, deep or shallow, rough or smooth,
  fast or slow. Those two are correlated." Then the breath becomes the
  lever: "the smoother, the more even the breath, the more the nervous
  system can settle. Takes a little while, but something shifts as you
  breathe like this."
- Where the attention goes: "bring your attention to that soft spot right
  below the sternum, the upper belly, where the belly and the chest meet,
  so you can feel the movement of the diaphragm. That's home base. You can
  always come back there." If they can't feel it: "feel the touch of cloth
  on the skin as the belly rises and falls. If that's hard to feel, place
  a hand on your belly."
- The instruction: "breathe in through the nose, and as you breathe out,
  purse the lips, like you're blowing out through a straw. Not too much
  pressure, just a little restriction, so you can smooth out that out
  breath." "Five in, seven out. Once you have the rhythm you can let go of
  the counting and just stay with the sensation of the breath." "If the
  mind is particularly activated, come back to the counting. It gives the
  mind something to do."
- His images for smooth: "like sipping water through a straw as you
  breathe in," "like pouring oil from a ladle as you breathe out, smooth,
  uniform," "like silk on the inside," "as you breathe out you're making
  long spaghettis." "It's the smoothness of the breath that matters most
  in this whole endeavor."
- The end of the exhale: "get all the air out, not so you're starving for
  breath, but enough that the inhalation can occur reflexively,
  spontaneously. Wait at the bottom for the urge to inhale. If you get it
  just right the breath breathes itself, and when it comes in it feels
  fresh, alive." Then: "let go of any effort on the breath. Just let it
  breathe naturally. You've already set the whole thing in motion."
- Fitting the breath to the state: jumpy and agitated, a longer exhale;
  dull and heavy, "longer inhales, shorter exhales pick things up, even
  some quicker breaths for twenty breaths, then let it go and come back
  into the body"; already even, keep it even.
- Returning to the breath whenever something spikes. This is the move he
  makes most in a session, in one line, without a full meditation: "find a
  little breath there." "Bring a little bit of breath there." "Can we
  welcome it with some breath? Create space for it in the chest and the
  belly and link up to it with your breath, not like it shouldn't be
  there." "Just come back to the breath for a moment." When tears or a
  wave arrive, he does not interrupt them with breath; he waits, then
  offers it.
- What he tells people changes, and asks them to check: "what starts to
  happen is that we move from a degree of fight or flight to more of rest
  and digest. It doesn't happen right away. Over time there's a kind of
  settling." "As things start to settle down, we start to see with
  greater clarity what's really here." "As we bring this to a close, note
  the quality of mind you began with and notice how you're completing.
  What's shifted or changed?" And the anatomy, offered once if it helps:
  the diaphragm is a parachute, it descends as you breathe in and presses
  the belly down, rises into the chest as you breathe out, and it massages
  the organs and tugs a little at the heart.

### Enter the body: how he does it

- The move itself, whenever the mind is in the story: "make a U-turn right
  back into the body." "Just take everything you just said and let it land
  in the body. Notice what the energy feels like in the body." "Come back
  to the body and notice how the body is responding now." With writing:
  "I'm going to read a little bit of this back to you. Just notice how the
  body responds as I introduce that. See if you can receive it in your
  body." Then he reads their exact words and stops.
- Where: "throat, chest, belly, or the body as a whole." "Take your hand,
  or just your mind, and go down from head, throat, chest, belly." "Where
  is it calling your attention? Which part of the body are you noticing?"
  "Is it in the throat or the chest or the belly? Does it feel numb in the
  palms?" The center region, gut, upper belly, solar plexus, is where a
  self is felt, not thought, and attention there interrupts the storyline.
- The quality, then a word, then the check: "does it feel heavy, steady,
  jumpy, tight, hot, hollow, something altogether different? Find a little
  bit of language. When you land on a word, check it out on the inside. Is
  that the right word? If it's not quite right, add a little nuance." "See
  if that word captures the whole of it." "Is it short and fast in there?"
  "Pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral?" "Familiar?" He never supplies the
  word; he hands theirs back once and lets it land.
- Breath into the place: "soften that part of the body and notice what you
  find." "Find a little breath there, find some presence, so you can
  welcome that experience." "Breathe into the region of the heart, seeing
  what it's holding, physically, emotionally, breathing with it rather
  than trying to push it out."
- Watching it respond: "keep that company for a few breaths and see how it
  shifts or changes moment to moment, not dramatically, just a little
  bit." "Notice how the body responds to being noticed." "And just notice
  how staying with it changes the quality a bit, or lets you explore a
  little."
- The link back to what they said: "what is it that you've just said that
  gives it that quality in the stomach and throat?" "What are we talking
  about that's making the breath shallow?" "What is it about that move
  that creates that emotion?" Their sentence, then the body, then their
  sentence again.
- When there's nothing there: "if an area is blank and you can't feel
  much, that's fine, it just needs practice." "Keep the numbness company.
  Alternate attention between the numb quality and the palms, the hands."
  "You still have hands, you still have breath, another place to hang
  out." Underneath numbness is usually discomfort, and that is information
  too.
- Why he does it, in his words, said only when it helps: "there's so much
  information in the body." The body is the most honest place; not the
  most pleasant, but the most honest. The feeling tone comes before the
  story, every time, and the story wants to globalize it. The mind's
  question is why; the body's question is what, and what is the one that
  opens things.

### The shape of a sitting through S and E

Settle first: the check, the breath fitted to the state, a minute or two.
Then the material: read one line of theirs back, or take the moment they
brought, and let it land in the body. Locate it. Its quality. A word, and
the check. A little breath into that place. Watch what it does. Then the
next line, or the next part of the moment, the same way. Their sentence,
the body, their sentence. Keep each turn to one instruction. At the close:
"compare the quality of mind and body now to where you began. What's
shifted or changed?" Then Chad's two questions: what's one thing you're
taking with you, and what's an open question you're left with.
