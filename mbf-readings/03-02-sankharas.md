# Sankharas

I said in the Welcome that something fires before the part climbs into the seat. Here's what that actually looks like, second by second.

A meeting shows up on your calendar you weren't expecting. Vibrations on a screen.

Before you've read the invite your toes curl, your stomach knots, your chest tightens. The mind hasn't arrived yet to explain anything, and the body has already declared this unpleasant.

That's the moment a sankhara starts to fire.

A sankhara is a deeply etched pattern of reactivity in your system. The word is Sanskrit, from a Buddhist psychology that mapped what happens during a triggered moment thousands of years before neuroscience caught up. The image is a groove. Like a line carved into wet concrete that hardened over time. Every time the same kind of trigger lands, you run the same groove. The groove gets deeper. The reaction becomes more automatic. Eventually it happens so fast that you don't notice it as a reaction at all. You just notice that you've done what you always do.

Most of the patterns that run you are like this. The ones formed in childhood. The ones from before you had language. Some of them aren't even yours. Research has shown that mice exposed to a smell paired with a shock pass on a fear response to that smell across multiple generations of offspring who were never shocked themselves. Trauma encoded in nervous systems can move forward through bloodlines. Some of what runs you today belongs to your ancestors. You can stop the line from continuing through you. You can't help having inherited it.

I want to be clear about something here. I've done a lot of therapy. I believe in therapy. It gave me language. It helped me understand my past. But understanding never changed the pattern. Awareness did. Insight showed me the blueprint. Awareness is what let me walk out of the house.

## The Five Steps

The Buddha mapped a triggered moment as five steps, and they happen in fractions of a second, which is why from the inside the whole thing feels like one event. It isn't. It's a sequence, and a sequence has places in it where you can get a hand in, if you can see them.

The five run left to right: contact, then feeling tone, then story, then reaction, then spinning. Each one hands off to the next. The opening to interrupt sits at the second one, feeling tone.

I'll walk you through them with one client's, because the meeting invite at the top of this chapter was his. He's been meditating fifteen years and he knows exactly what he's in when it happens. He calls it *dukkha*. I'll call him Paul.

### 1. Contact (Phassa)

The trigger lands, arriving through one of your senses. A sound, a face, a text, an email. For Paul it was a meeting appearing on his calendar that he hadn't been expecting, which is to say vibrations on a screen and nothing more than that.

### 2. Feeling Tone (Vedana)

The body responds before the mind does, and a wave of pleasant or unpleasant or neutral arrives that you didn't choose and can't have chosen, because it's already there.

Paul described it as a canoe with a sharp edge cutting through his brain, running the groove it always runs. Toes curled. Stomach knotted. Chest tight. All of that before he'd read a word of the invite.

And this is the first place the loop could break. It's also the place almost nobody notices.

### 3. Story (Sañña)

Now the mind arrives with an explanation, pulling from memory to assign meaning to what the body already did, and it feels true the second it appears because it's been filtered through decades of conditioning.

Paul's story: an unexpected meeting means he's being laid off.

It might be right and it might be wrong, and the loop doesn't care either way. What matters is that the body's signal now has an interpretation attached, and from here on the system reacts to the interpretation as though it were the thing itself.

### 4. Reaction (Sankhara)

This is where the system acts, and Paul put it better than I could: a switch gets thrown somewhere below his control. He's numb, he's hiding, he's doom-scrolling, he's reaching for an edible, he's over-complicating a work project so the charge has somewhere to go.

His phrase for the whole move is *stay away from the pain.*

And this is what most people mean when they say reaction. By the time you can see it, it's too late to do anything else.

### 5. Proliferation (Papancha)

Then the mind keeps spinning long after the reaction is done. Rumination, shame, replay, worst cases.

Paul calls what he ends up in *a prison of my own creation*.

And the whole run, contact to spinning, takes about a second.

## Two Flavors of the Same Loop

There's another client of mine, I'll call him Raymond, whose loop is structurally identical to Paul's but ends in the opposite direction.

Something lands sideways at work, and he's left off a project, or the room goes uncertain, or a sign reads as a threat. (Contact.)

It shows up in his body first, every single time. A heavy steel ball in his belly and a suffocating pressure in his chest. (Feeling tone, unpleasant.)

The mind translates it into a verdict that's older than the job. He's lesser than. He's going to be forgotten and left behind. His worth is only his utility. He spins what he calls *conspiracy theories about his value*. (Story.)

Then he moves. He jimmies up solutions. He hops environments. He chases the next title, the next bogie, on the belief that the next milestone will finally make him a somebody. (Reaction.)

That's the contrast I want you holding, because it's the same four stages with the fourth move pointing the opposite way. Paul's reaction is aversion: numb, hide, get away from it. Raymond's is craving: go and get the thing that will finally make it stop.

Same chain. And neither one of them ever touches the steel ball.

## The Sankhara is the Contract Firing

Let me tie this back to Module 1.

In *Why We Perform* I named the **contract**, the unspoken deal your nervous system signed before you had words for it: worth has to be earned, parts of me get left behind to stay connected. And I named what happens when something now registers as enough like the old conditions to bring the old danger back, which I called **time travel**.

A sankhara is what time travel looks like from inside the body.

Paul's loop, with the calendar invite, is the contract enforcing itself. Somewhere underneath the doom-scrolling and the edible, the contract is saying *if I'm being seen, I'm in trouble*. That sentence was true once, in some room a long time ago. The sankhara fires now because the system can't yet tell that the old room is gone.

Raymond's loop, with the project he was left off, is the same contract wearing the other costume. *If I'm not the indispensable one, I don't get to stay.* That was also true once. The sankhara fires now to make sure he doesn't find out what happens if he stops chasing.

Every sankhara you can map is the contract trying to do the job it signed up for forty years ago. Mapping it is the first move toward firing it.

## The Kid and the Sankhara

You may be wondering how this squares with the kids on the bus, so let me be plain: it's one thing seen from two angles. Every kid you named is running a sankhara. The kid is the actor and the sankhara is the loop the actor has been running for years.

So which lens you reach for depends on what you need. When you want to know who's driving, use the bus. When you want to see the structure of what you're caught in, use this. And the entry point is the only real difference. In Module 2 you came in by naming the part. Here you come in through the body.

## Where the Loop Breaks

So you can see where the opening is.

The reaction is too late, because by the time you're doom-scrolling or chasing the next title the thing has already fired. And arguing with the story is too late as well, because by the time you can challenge the layoff thought the body has locked in and the reaction is already underway.

The opening is at the feeling tone. The curling toes. The steel ball. Both of them arrive before the story and before the reaction, and both sit in the system for a beat, sometimes a long one, before the switch flips.

That beat is the whole of it. Not arguing with the layoff thought, not talking yourself out of jimmying. Feeling the body before the mind gets there, and staying with it while it's there.

This is what the next chapter, Be With, teaches as a practice.

## Summary

- A sankhara is a deeply etched loop of reactivity. It runs the same groove every time the same kind of trigger lands.
- The loop has five steps: Contact (the trigger), Feeling Tone (the body's response), Story (the meaning the mind assigns), Reaction (the conditioned move), and Proliferation (the mental spinning that follows).
- Two flavors of the same loop. Aversion goes numb and hides. Craving chases the thing that will make the feeling stop. Same four stages, opposite fourth move.
- The loop breaks at the feeling tone. By the time you see the reaction or the story, you're too late. The opening is earlier, in the body, before the mind gets there.
