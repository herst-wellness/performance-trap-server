# Welcome

Module 1 ended on beginner's mind, which I described as setting down, even for a minute, the lens you've been looking through your whole life, so that something you've been missing has a chance to reach you.

Here's the question I want to open with. If you can set the lens down, who exactly is doing the setting down?

Because you can't put a thing down while you are it, which means something in you has to not be the lens, and that something is what this whole module is about. Once you can find it, everything else I give you here follows from it.

So beginner's mind is what you do. What I'm handing you now is what does it.

## Awareness

What I mean by awareness is the still, quiet attention behind the thoughts and the stories. It doesn't judge and it doesn't take sides. It notices.

The shift sounds small and it changes everything. *I am anxious* becomes *there's anxiety here*, and the feeling stops being all of who you are and becomes something moving through. That's the move I named a minute ago, in its smallest possible form: something that was me becoming a part of me.

And the part of you doing the noticing isn't something new you're building. It's the self you came in with, before you learned to contort yourself to belong, and it's been sitting underneath the whole time.

## What Awareness Lets You See

Here's why that matters, and it's the hinge of the whole module.

When you can look at a story instead of through it, you notice something that wasn't available before. The story had an author.

I don't think that's a small observation. For most of your life the story about who you are has arrived feeling like a report on reality, something you were simply told by the world and then confirmed. Awareness lets you catch it in the act of being written. And the moment you can catch it being written, you can ask who's doing the writing.

## The Ego, the Author

What usually answers *who am I?* before you've had a chance to look is the ego, your inner storyteller, keeping a running narrative. I'm the competent one. I'm the strong one. I'm the one who can be relied on. It pulls your memories and what people have said about you into a self that feels true.

The ego isn't bad, it's how you get through the world. But it gets attached to its own story, and when something challenges that story it tightens its grip until the story feels like the truth. And while you're inside it, you can't see it. Beginner's mind is the crowbar.

## Why the Story Got Written

Which raises the next question. If the ego wrote a story, what was it writing against?

In Module 1 I showed you the Still Face, where an infant reaches, gets nothing back, and settles itself. That teaches your nervous system to handle things alone. But something else keeps you in the relationship anyway, and it's called the double bind.

The term comes from the anthropologist Gregory Bateson and his group at Palo Alto in the 1950s, studying how families communicate. What they found was a trap that happens when two channels contradict each other and you can't name the contradiction without consequences.

Picture a manager who's completely overwhelmed. Eyes glazed, you can feel the cortisol coming off them. And they say: my door is always open, tell me what's wrong.

The words invite you in. The body screams *I cannot handle one more thing*.

Trust the words and walk in, you might set them off. Name the body language, you become the problem. There's no clean move, and you can't leave or call it out or relax into either signal. So you do the only thing left. You adjust. You shrink your reaction, smooth your tone, and keep the appearance of connection intact by overriding what you feel.

The same thing happened to you repeatedly as a child. A parent who said *I'm fine, everything's okay*, with tight shoulders and a thin voice. Not angry. Overwhelmed, barely holding. You heard okay and you felt how stretched they were, and if you'd brought your fear you'd have added to the weight. If you'd said *you don't seem okay*, you might have been told you were overthinking.

So you adjusted. You got easier, quieter, more capable.

And here's the cost, which is what I want you holding when you read the rest of this. You stopped trusting your own reading of a room. Not the words, and not your gut either, because neither one could be relied on, so you needed something else to go on.

**That's what the ego was writing against.** The story is what you got instead of trust in your own perception. It told you who to be when you couldn't tell what was true.

## The Shapes the Story Took

Those stories aren't infinite, and in my experience they take three shapes. You'll recognize at least one of them as yours, and most likely two.

Achievement. If I win the award, build the company, hit the number, then I'll finally belong in the room. The people I see didn't become high achievers because they love praise. They chase the gold star to calm themselves down, and if they can do it perfectly they never have to risk needing anything from anyone. It doesn't touch the ache, but for a few hours it quiets things.

Care. If I solve your problems, manage your emotions, anticipate what you need, then I won't be left behind. The fixer, the one who feels the room shift before anyone speaks and moves in to steady it. Being needed is how they stay close, and if everyone else is all right they can finally breathe.

Invulnerability. If I never cry, never need anything, never burden anyone, then I'm safe. This one says I'm fine and disappears when things get intense. Not texting back, not asking, not making a sound. Not because there are no needs, but because handling it alone turned out to be safer.

Different costumes. I find the same lesson underneath all three. Don't count on being met. Make safety through control.

Most people I work with blend two, achievement at work and invulnerability at home, or care with friends and invulnerability with a partner. And none of it is a flaw. These were intelligent adaptations to the conditions you were in, and they kept connection possible when the alternative felt like annihilation.

## The Kids on the Bus

So you have a story, and you have the shape it took. What's left is the question of who keeps it running day to day, and this is the part I most want you to see.

Because I've never seen anyone maintain a story like that alone. You're a collection of parts, each with a voice and an agenda. The inner critic that points out your flaws. The pleaser that bends over backward. The scared kid who wants comfort. The good kid who wants to make it look easy. I call them the kids on the bus, and they're the crew keeping the ego's story on the road.

You'd think the driver is some unified you. In my experience it's whichever kid is loudest at the time, and the critic is usually loudest of all. Most people don't hear it as one part among others. They hear it as themselves, which is exactly why I started you with awareness.

## What's Coming

Three chapters, in that same order. Awareness, as something you practice rather than something you have. The Kids on the Bus, where you meet your own crew. And Parts as Protectors, which is the bit most people don't expect: every one of them has a job, and none of them is a problem to get rid of.

The breath is still the base, because I can't get anyone here from inside fight-or-flight. That hasn't changed since Module 1.
