# Parts as Protectors

You can name the kids now. Which raises the question that decides whether any of this helps: once you can see a part, what do you actually do with it?

Most people's instinct is to get rid of it, and that instinct is the thing I want to take off you here.

Here's the move everything else in this work turns on. Every part has positive intent. The anxious one, the angry one, and the inner critic that's been telling you you're not enough for as long as you can remember. Each took on a job at some point and each is still doing it, so what I'm asking you for isn't to silence them or push them out. It's to meet them as what they are, which is protectors.

And I mean that word exactly. Not nuisances, not weaknesses, not the bits of you to fix. They came online because a younger version of you needed protecting.

## What Resistance Does

The first thing most of us do with an uncomfortable part is fight it. The anxious one is unbearable so we suppress it. The angry one embarrasses us so we lock it down. The critic is exhausting so we argue back.

And I've never seen any of it work. Jung had the line for it: what you resist persists.

I watched it in Maya's Rabbit, where the harder she fought the louder it got. That frantic anxiety wasn't asking to be defeated, it was asking to be acknowledged, and it hopped harder every time she pushed it away. Once she stopped fighting and turned toward it, it sat down.

That's how parts work. They want recognition, not approval. Denied it they escalate, and given it they usually quiet.

## What They're Actually Protecting

Look closely at any of them and what they're protecting turns out not to be abstract. It's specific, and it's a tender younger part of you that learned somewhere that being hurt or disappointed or left was unbearable.

The Rabbit isn't generating anxiety for its own sake. It's scanning the horizon so a girl who was once blindsided never has to be again. The Firefighter isn't manic for the love of action. It works that hard because it believes that if it stops the bottom falls out. And the Hulk isn't furious for sport. It's standing at the boundary of someone whose dignity has been violated before and who learned to keep that hidden.

So underneath every protector is a younger self with a particular vulnerability, and however loud or exhausting or self-destructive the strategy looks from outside, the job is real.

Which is what makes treating a part as the enemy so backwards. Fighting the protector doesn't free the tender one underneath. It leaves them undefended while the protector works harder to cover the gap.

So the question I'd have you hold is *who is this part trying to keep safe?* Sometimes you'll sense who, and often, at first, you won't. But it's a different question from *why won't this thing stop*, and I find it goes somewhere different.

## The Move

Maya's three needed three different meetings and responded to all of them, which is the pattern. There are four steps in it.

**1. Unblend.** Notice the part as a part rather than as you, so that *I'm anxious* becomes *some part of me is anxious*. That's the move you practiced in the last journal, and it makes the distance you need in order to meet the thing instead of being it.

**2. Stop resisting.** Whatever fight you've been having with it, put it down for a minute. I'm not asking you to like the part or agree with it. Only to stop pushing it away, because the pushing is what's feeding it.

**3. Find the positive intent.** Ask what it's trying to protect, and then ask who. In my experience the answer is usually a younger version of you with a particular vulnerability, and once you can name who's underneath, the behavior makes sense to you even while it's still costing you.

**4. Meet it on its terms.** The form depends on the part. Some need parenting, the way the Rabbit did: *I got you. I know you're scared.* Some need negotiating with in their own logic, the way the Firefighter did: *We're not stopping, we're resting to protect the long game.* Some need their strength acknowledged and felt without being acted on, the way the Hulk did: *I see how strong you are. We were disrespected.* What they have in common is that they need contact, not combat, and all of them benefit from being thanked. *I see what you've been doing. I know you've been trying to keep me safe. Thank you for that. We can do this differently now.*

## The Inner Critic

I find the critic the hardest of all of them to meet this way, because it feels less like a kid on the bus than like a verdict, and what it says is often sharp enough that the impulse to fight back is hard to override. I still get caught by mine.

But it's a protector too, and a clumsy one, usually trying to keep you safe by getting to your flaws before anyone else can. *If I lower your expectations, the world's rejection won't sting as much. If I criticize you first, you can fix it before it's exposed. If I keep you small, you won't be a target.*

That logic came from a much younger you and it was useful once, keeping you out of trouble in a household or a schoolyard where standing out was costly and needing too much got punished. It's still running because nobody ever told it the threat was over.

So it doesn't need arguing with. It needs hearing. *I see what you're trying to do. You're trying to keep me safe, and I appreciate it, and the rules you're using are forty years out of date.*

I won't pretend that's a cure. It's how I've seen the grip start to loosen, slowly.

## Why Compassion Is the Move

One misunderstanding I want to head off. Compassion sounds soft, as though turning toward a part with warmth means letting it off the hook.

I don't find it soft at all. You can hold a part responsible while still meeting it as a protector. *I see what you're trying to do, and I'm not going to let you keep running my life this way.*

So none of them are your enemies. Not the critic, not the Rabbit, not the pleaser or the perfectionist or the scared kid. They're exhausted guardians who've been doing a hard job for a long time, and what they need is to be thanked for it, told the threat is over, and offered a more sustainable way to help.

## Before You Put This Down

Look away and see how many of these you can say back. Getting them wrong is fine and still does the work.

- Every part has positive intent, and underneath it is a younger you with a particular vulnerability.
- Fighting a part makes it louder. What you resist persists.
- The four moves: unblend, stop resisting, find the positive intent, meet it on its terms.
- Different parts need different meetings. Parented, negotiated with, or acknowledged without being acted on.
- The critic is hardest because it sounds like a verdict, and it's a protector too.

## Action

After your daily breath practice, work through **02.04A Meeting a Part Journal**. You'll pick one of the kids you identified in the last journal and have a real encounter with it, walking the four moves on a single specific part.

And through your day, when you notice a part has taken the wheel, try not to fight it. Just ask what it's trying to protect. I don't need you to get a complete answer. The question itself is the move.
