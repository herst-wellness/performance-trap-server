# Expand Options

*This is **E** in **STEP**: expand options. The third move of the outer half. The widening that becomes possible once the trade has been tuned in to.*

A client of mine, I'll call him Evan, came in frustrated about a colleague named Ken. Ken had been showing up late. He'd dropped a couple of customer emails. Evan was sure Ken was lazy.

He'd been collecting evidence. Late arrivals. Sloppy follow-through. He'd even cornered Ken's manager at the coffee station and gotten agreement: *yeah, he's lazy.* A few days before our session, Evan saw Ken working overtime, and his first thought was, *who's he trying to fool. Everyone knows he's a lazy guy.*

I asked Evan if he'd talked to Ken about any of this. He hadn't.

Then I told him what I'd learned a few weeks earlier from someone who knew Ken better than Evan did.

Ken's wife was dying of cancer. She was in the hospital. He had three young children at home, two of them under six, trying to make sense of why their mother kept disappearing for treatment. He was distracted at work because his life had been split open.

Evan went quiet for a while. Then he said, *I made up a whole story about that guy.*

That's the move this chapter teaches. Not the part where Evan finds out he was wrong. The part where he realizes he was making up a story in the first place.

## Story Versus Fact

Most of what we call thinking is storytelling.

When something happens, we don't just register it. We interpret it. We weave the disparate pieces, what someone said, how they looked when they said it, what they did the day before, into a coherent narrative that explains what's going on. The narrative feels like the truth. It feels like we're seeing reality. We're not. We're seeing our interpretation of reality, dressed up as reality.

This isn't a flaw. It's a feature. The brain is a prediction machine. It builds stories because stories let us anticipate what's coming. Without that capacity, we'd be paralyzed by every new piece of input.

The problem is that we forget we're doing it. We treat the story as the situation. And then we act on the story, and the story gets confirmed, because the way we act tends to produce the response we predicted.

Evan was sure Ken was lazy. He treated him accordingly. Ken, who was barely holding his life together, got the message that his colleague had written him off. The relationship went exactly the way you'd expect.

The teaching here is simple. You can almost always separate the story from the facts. And you almost never do it on your own.

## How to Actually Do It

When you find yourself stuck, locked in a conflict that's going nowhere, paralyzed about a decision, sure of how something is going to play out, try this.

Get a piece of paper. Two columns.

**Left column: what are the facts?** Just what happened, what was said, what's observable. Pretend you're a court reporter. No interpretation, no motive, no story. Just the record.

**Right column: what's the story you're telling about those facts?** Why did he do it? What does it mean about her? What's going to happen next?

Most people, the first time they try this, can't separate them. The story is so welded to the facts that the facts disappear. You'll find yourself writing *she didn't care about my feelings* in the left column. That's not a fact. That's a conclusion. The fact is *she didn't respond when I said I was upset.* The conclusion is the story.

Once you've actually pulled them apart, look at the story side. Notice that nothing on that side is verified. It's all extrapolation. Often it's extrapolation built on a foundation of evidence you cherry-picked because it confirmed what you already feared.

This isn't a trick to get you to think positive thoughts. The story might be accurate. Sometimes the person actually is acting in bad faith. Sometimes you actually are about to be passed over again. Separating story from fact doesn't mean the story is wrong. It means you can hold the story with appropriate humility while you go check.

## Widening the Aperture

Once you know you're working with a story rather than the truth, you can try out other stories.

Sarah came to me a few months ago. She'd been on a few dates with a man named John. They were planning a trip to Mexico. That morning, she'd gotten a text saying he couldn't go anymore. No explanation.

By the time she arrived for our session, she'd built a complete story. He was losing interest. She'd done something wrong on the last date. She was about to get ghosted, the same way she had been by the previous guy she'd dated. Her chest was tight. She was rehearsing how to write him off before he could write her off.

I asked her to tell me the facts. She managed two.

*He sent a text. He said he couldn't go on the trip.*

That's all she actually knew.

Then I asked her what other stories were possible. At first, she couldn't think of any. The losing-interest story had taken over the available space. After a few minutes, she came up with: maybe a work thing came up. Maybe he was nervous about the trip itself, not about her. Maybe something happened with his family. Maybe he was bad at texting and didn't realize the message came across as cold.

I asked which story her body wanted to be true.

*Obviously, the not-losing-interest ones.*

*Which one is most likely to be true?*

*I have no idea. Honestly, any of them could be.*

*Right. So what would you actually like to know?*

*Why can't he go. Whether he's still in this.*

That was the first time in our session that she sounded like herself instead of like her Inner Critic. She wrote back that afternoon and asked. He told her his sister had just been hospitalized, and he was flying home that weekend. He apologized for not explaining in the original text. They went on the trip a month later.

This isn't a story about Sarah being right to assume good intent. The point isn't that John turned out to have a sympathetic reason. The point is that Sarah was acting on a story she'd built without information, and the story had her on the verge of ending the relationship preemptively. The widening of the aperture didn't tell her what was true. It got her to the question she actually needed to ask.

She might have asked and learned that John was, in fact, pulling away. That would have been hard, but it would have been real. Either way, she'd be working with the actual situation rather than with the story she'd manufactured to protect herself.

## Mindsets as Different Lenses

Sometimes the way you widen the aperture isn't by listing alternative stories. It's by looking at the situation through a different mindset.

You've worked with a lot of mindsets in this training. Beginner's Mind. Equanimity. Self-compassion. The Inner Critic. Impermanence. Each one is a lens. Each one brings certain features of a situation into focus and lets others recede.

If you frame a hard conversation through Beginner's Mind, the question becomes *what do I not yet know about this?* If you frame it through the Inner Critic, the question becomes *what is this voice in my head trying to protect me from?* If you frame it through impermanence, the question becomes *is this thing I'm so worried about going to matter in a year?*

None of these is the right frame. The point is that having a frame, deliberately chosen, is better than being stuck inside one you didn't choose. The default frame is usually the one your conditioning hands you, which is usually the one that's keeping you stuck.

The working list of mindsets lives in **07.03B Glossary of Mindsets**. Read it before you do the journal. Notice which ones you respond to. Two or three will probably feel alive. Those are the ones to use.

## The Inner Board of Trustees

There's one frame worth naming specifically because it does so much of the heavy lifting in Module 7. Your heartfelt intentions and your life purpose statements aren't just things to live up to. They're frames you can use to look at any stuck situation. When you're not sure which way to move, ask which option honors what you said matters to you.

In the last chapter you met David, the leader caught between absorbing his wife's anger and pushing back in a way that escalates the fight. His third option was to consult his inner board of trustees. The four values he'd named in Module 6 (humor, resilience, integrity, perspective) gave him a way to approach the conflict that wasn't either collapse or counterattack. He'd pause inside the fight and ask: *which part of my deeper board needs to speak here?*

That's the practical use of what you wrote in Module 6. Your heartfelt intentions and life purpose are decision-making instruments under pressure. When the binary collapses your world to two options, the board reminds you that there are more truths in play than the two the bind made visible.

When Sarah looked at John's text through the lens of her heartfelt intention around connection, her chest opened. From the opening, she could write a real message. From the closed-off place, she could only write a closed-off message, or no message at all. The frame her intention provided was the third option in disguise.

## Why This Works on the Body

A frame isn't just a thought. It's a felt experience.

When you tell yourself the story that your partner is being cruel, your chest tightens. When you tell yourself the story that your partner is overwhelmed and protecting themselves badly, your chest softens. The facts in the room haven't changed. Your body's response has, because it's responding to the story, not the facts.

This is also why *just think positively* doesn't work. You can't talk your body into a frame your nervous system doesn't believe. The work is to find a frame that's honest, possibly true, and that lets your body release. From that release, a new action becomes available.

The body is the test. If a frame doesn't change anything in how you feel, it's not the right frame yet. Keep looking.

For the science behind why this works (your brain as a prediction machine, your heartfelt intentions as new seeds that change what you predict and how you act), listen to the recording in your audio folder: *How the Brain Constructs Reality.* It's about twenty minutes. You don't have to listen to it to do the work, but it grounds the practice in what's actually happening in the nervous system.

## Intuition

So far we've been talking about widening the aperture above the neck. Stories, mindsets, frames. The thinking mind's work.

There's another part of you that already knows. It lives below the neck. The gut. The heart. The body's network of internal sensing.

For a long time, the West treated intelligence as something that lived in the head. The rest of you was just the equipment that carried your brain around. That's backwards. Your gut has roughly five hundred million neurons, about the same as a cat's brain. It produces over ninety-five percent of the serotonin in your body. The insula, the part of your brain that takes in everything happening inside you, turns the signals into the felt sense of being alive. The heart has between forty and a hundred and twenty thousand neurons of its own. It sends more information up to the brain than the brain sends down to it.

What we call intuition is your access to all of that. The body's intelligence, communicating with the part of your brain that knows what's actually going on, often before your thinking mind has caught up.

The thinking mind argues. It debates. It lists pros and cons. It runs scenarios. Intuition doesn't do any of that. Intuition is declarative. It says yes or no, here or not here, now or wait. It doesn't justify itself.

That's part of why it's easy to dismiss. Most of us have been trained to trust whatever can defend itself in an argument, and intuition won't argue. It just says the thing.

Sometimes intuition speaks in words. A short phrase that arrives without context. *Go now. Don't sign this. Call her.* The Quakers have a name for this: the still small voice within. Most people who've lived long enough have had at least one moment when they heard it, ignored it, and regretted ignoring it.

More often, intuition speaks in the body. Your stomach tightens when you walk into the room. Your shoulders drop when you read a particular sentence. Your throat closes around a word you're about to say. These aren't random. They're the body registering information your conscious mind hasn't gotten to yet.

The challenge is that the same channels carry old fear, old conditioning, and old protectiveness alongside genuine knowing. The shoulder tightening might be intuition saying *this isn't safe.* It might also be a learned reaction from a situation that ended thirty years ago. The work isn't to trust every body signal. The work is to get familiar enough with the signals that you can tell which is which.

That familiarity comes from time. There's no shortcut.

## How to Practice Listening

When you have a question you're sitting with, something you can't think your way through, find a quiet place. Sit. Take a few breaths. Drop your attention into the body.

Hold the question in your awareness. Not as a problem to solve. Just as a question. *Should I take this job? Is this relationship worth staying in? Do I need to make this call?*

Don't try to think about it. Just let the question sit there, and notice what happens in your body.

What you're looking for is movement. A softening somewhere. A tightening somewhere. The breath getting deeper or shallower. The throat opening or closing. A sensation that wasn't there before.

Once you notice something, try the inverse. If you've been holding the question *should I take this job,* now hold *I'm taking this job.* Notice what shifts. Then hold *I'm not taking this job.* Notice what shifts.

You're not asking your mind which one is right. You're asking your body which one feels alive and which one feels dead. The yes from intuition tends to feel like opening, expansion, a small forward lean. The no tends to feel like contraction, heaviness, a small recoil.

Sometimes you'll get a clear signal. Sometimes you'll get nothing, or conflicting signals from different parts of the body. That's information too. It usually means you don't have enough to decide yet, or that there's something underneath the question you haven't gotten to.

## When the Body Lies, and When It Doesn't

A fair question: how do you know your gut is giving you real information rather than recycling old fear?

The honest answer is that you don't always know, and you have to develop a feel for the difference over time.

Old fear tends to feel familiar. It has a quality of *I've been here before.* The contraction matches a contraction you remember from your childhood, your last relationship, the pattern you've been running for years. If you sit with it, you can usually trace it back to a specific origin.

Real intuition tends to feel impersonal. It doesn't have your story attached to it. It's just information arriving, neutral, a clean signal. The yes or the no doesn't come with a defense.

The other test is time. Intuition tends to be steady. The signal you got yesterday is still there today, and was there last week. Reactive fear tends to fluctuate. It spikes when you're tired, recedes when you've eaten, and comes back at night. If a body signal stays consistent across moods and days, take it seriously.

## Where This Comes From

A brief lineage note worth carrying with you.

The work of separating story from fact comes out of cognitive science, but also out of the contemplative traditions that have been training the move for centuries. Brother David Steindl-Rast, whose Stop-Look-Go practice we've been using, is one teacher in this lineage. The constructivist neuroscience of Lisa Feldman Barrett and others gives the same teaching in the language of the brain. Both arrive at the same place: the felt sense of certainty isn't proof of accuracy. It's proof that the brain has built a story that feels like truth.

The intuition piece has a different lineage. Eugene Gendlin called this kind of body-knowing the *felt sense,* and developed an entire practice (he called it Focusing) around contacting it. The yoga traditions I learned from teach this directly. The body has its own intelligence. The practice is to make space for it to speak.

You don't need to remember the names. I'm telling you so you know the practice has weight behind it.

## Summary

- Most stuck situations aren't stuck because of facts. They're stuck because of the story we've built around the facts.
- Separate fact from story. The fact is what a court reporter would write down. The story is everything else.
- Once you know you're working with a story, try other stories. Notice which one your body responds to.
- Mindsets, and your heartfelt intentions and life purpose, are also frames. Use them deliberately when the default frame is keeping you stuck.
- Intuition is your access to the body's intelligence. It speaks declaratively, often in sensation. Practice on small questions so the listening is built when something big arrives.
- The body is the test. A real reframe shows up as a release somewhere inside you.
