# Expand Options

*This is **E** in **STEP**: expand options. The widening that becomes possible once you can see the trade.*

The last chapter said the third option is invisible because something in you learned it wasn't allowed. Which leaves the practical problem, and it's the one I get asked about most. How do you find it while you're inside the narrowing, when the bind is doing its job and the field feels like it holds exactly two things?

So that's what I want to give you here, and I'm going to start somewhere that will look like a detour.

A client I'll call Evan came in frustrated about a colleague. The man had been arriving late and had dropped a couple of customer emails, and Evan was certain he was lazy.

He'd been collecting evidence, and had even cornered the man's manager at the coffee station and got agreement. Then days before our session he saw him working overtime, and his first thought was *who's he trying to fool.*

I asked him whether he'd talked to the man about any of it. He hadn't.

So I told him what I'd learned from somebody who knew the man better than he did. His wife was in hospital with cancer, and he had three young children at home, two of them under six, trying to work out why their mother kept disappearing.

Evan went quiet for a while. Then he said, *I made up a whole story about that guy.*

That's the move I'm after. Not the part where he finds out he was wrong. The part where he sees he was making one up at all.

## Story Versus Fact

Most of what I'd call thinking is storytelling, mine included.

Something happens and we don't just register it, we interpret it. We weave the pieces together into a narrative that explains the whole thing, and the narrative feels like the truth. It feels like seeing reality, and it's seeing your interpretation of reality dressed up as reality.

And that isn't a flaw. Your brain is a prediction machine, and it builds stories so it can anticipate what's coming, and without that you'd be paralyzed by every new thing that happened to you.

The problem is that I forget I'm doing it, and so do you. We treat the story as the situation, act on it, and it gets confirmed, because the way we act tends to produce the response we predicted. Evan was sure the man was lazy and treated him that way, and the man, barely holding his life together, got the message he'd been written off.

So here's the move. You can almost always separate the story from the facts, and in my experience you almost never do it on your own.

## How to Do It

When you're stuck, in a conflict going nowhere or certain how something will play out, I want you to take a piece of paper and make two columns.

**The facts.** What happened, what was said, what's observable, as though you were a court reporter. No motive and no interpretation.

**The story.** Why did he do it? What does it mean about her? What happens next?

Most people can't do it the first time. The story is welded so tightly to the facts that the facts disappear, and you'll catch yourself writing *she didn't care about my feelings* on the left. That isn't a fact. The fact is *she didn't respond when I said I was upset.*

And then look at the story side and notice that none of it is verified. It's all extrapolation, and often extrapolation built on evidence you picked because it confirmed what you already feared.

I'm not trying to make you think positively. The story might be right. Sometimes the person really is acting in bad faith, and sometimes you really are about to be passed over. Separating them doesn't make it wrong. It lets you hold it with some humility while you go and check.

## Widening the Aperture

And once you know you're holding a story, you can try other ones.

I had a client who'd been on a few dates with a man she liked. They'd planned a trip, and that morning she had a text saying he couldn't go, with no explanation. By the time she sat down with me she'd built the whole thing. He was losing interest, she'd done something wrong, she was about to be ghosted the way she had been before. Her chest was tight and she was rehearsing how to write him off first.

I asked her for the facts and she managed two. He sent a text. He said he couldn't go.

Then I asked what other stories were possible, and at first she couldn't find any, because the losing-interest one had taken all the space. After a few minutes she got there. Maybe a work thing. Maybe he was nervous about the trip rather than about her. Maybe he's bad at texting.

I asked which was most likely.

*I have no idea. Any of them could be.*

So what would you actually like to know?

*Why he can't go. Whether he's still in this.*

That was the first time in the hour she'd sounded like herself rather than like her inner critic. She wrote and asked him that afternoon, and his sister had been hospitalized and he was flying home to her.

But the point isn't that he had a sympathetic reason. She might have asked and found he was pulling away, and that would have been hard and real. What the widening did wasn't tell her what was true. It got her to the question she needed to ask.

## Other Lenses

And sometimes you widen it not by listing stories but by looking through a different frame.

You've worked with a lot of them here. Beginner's mind, equanimity, self-compassion, the inner critic, impermanence. Each brings some features forward and lets others recede. Through beginner's mind the question becomes *what don't I know yet.* Through the critic, *what is this voice protecting me from.* Through impermanence, *will this matter in a year.*

None of them is the right one. What I'm after is that a frame you chose beats the one your conditioning handed you, which is usually the one keeping you stuck. The working list is in **Glossary of Mindsets**, and two or three of them will feel alive to you. Use those.

And I'd point at one particularly. What you wrote in Module 6 isn't only something to live up to. Your intentions and your purpose are frames, and when you don't know which way to move you can ask which option honors what you said matters. I've watched a client sit inside a fight with his wife and ask which part of his own board needed to speak, and come out with a move that was neither collapse nor counterattack.

## Why a Frame Moves the Body

A frame isn't only a thought. It's something you feel, and I'd test it that way.

Tell yourself your partner is being cruel and your chest tightens. Tell yourself your partner is overwhelmed and protecting themselves badly and your chest softens. Nothing in the room changed. Your body is responding to the story rather than the facts.

And it's why *think positively* doesn't work. You can't talk your body into a frame your nervous system doesn't believe. I want one that's honest, possibly true, and that lets something release. If it changes nothing in how you feel, it isn't the right one yet.

## Intuition

Everything I've given you so far is above the neck. Stories, frames, the thinking mind's work. But there's another part of you that already knows, and it lives lower down.

Your gut has around five hundred million neurons and makes most of the serotonin in your body, and your heart sends more up to your brain than it gets back. What I mean by intuition is your access to that.

The thinking mind argues and lists pros and cons. Intuition doesn't. It says yes or no, here or not here, now or wait, and it won't justify itself, which is exactly why I find people dismiss it. We were trained to trust whatever wins an argument.

Sometimes it comes in words with no context. *Don't sign this. Call her.* More often it's in the body, and your stomach tightens as you walk in.

The trouble is that the same channels carry old fear and old conditioning alongside real knowing. The tight shoulder might be saying this isn't safe, or it might be a reaction to something that ended thirty years ago.

Two tests help me. Old fear feels familiar, with a quality of *I've been here before*, and I can usually trace it back to somewhere specific. Real intuition feels impersonal, with none of my story attached to it. And it's steady, where reactive fear fluctuates and spikes when I'm tired. If a signal holds across moods and days, I take it seriously.

## How to Ask

So when you're sitting with something you can't think your way through, find somewhere quiet, drop your attention into the body, and hold the question. Not as a problem to solve. As a question. Then notice what moves.

Then try the inverse. Hold *I'm taking this job* and notice, then *I'm not taking this job* and notice. I'm not asking your mind which is right. I'm asking your body which one feels alive and which feels dead.

And sometimes you'll get nothing, or two parts of you saying different things, and I'd take that as information too. It usually means you don't have enough to decide on yet.

## Summary

- Most of what feels like thinking is storytelling, and it gets confirmed because you act on it.
- Two columns. Facts left, story right, and you almost never separate them alone.
- Widening doesn't tell you what's true. It gets you to the question you need to ask.
- A frame you chose beats the one your conditioning handed you.
- The body is the test. A frame that changes nothing isn't the right one yet.
- Old fear feels familiar and fluctuates. Intuition feels impersonal and holds steady.
