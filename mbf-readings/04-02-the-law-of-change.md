# The Law of Change

In the Welcome I said the protector only finds out it's wrong by living through a wave and still being here afterward. Which raises the obvious question: why would you believe it ends, while you're inside it?

So what I want to give you here is the one fact the whole capacity rests on, and the difference between knowing it and having felt it.

Everything changes, which is the most obvious thing in the world and almost impossible to live by.

Leaves go brittle and seasons turn, kids outgrow their clothes overnight, bodies age, relationships shift, jobs end, and moods come and go like weather. You've seen it a thousand times. And then the next thing you love starts to change, or the next thing you hate refuses to, and you react as though this one is the exception. You grip what feels good, hoping it might stay. You brace against what hurts, as if bracing could stop it arriving. You keep losing the same game.

The Buddha called this *anicca*, impermanence, and it's the thing most of what I teach rests on. Once your body actually knows it rather than agrees with it, the equation underneath your reactions changes. The bracing relaxes, the clinging loosens, the bargaining with reality stops, and you're left with what's here.

## The Wave and the Ocean

Stand on a beach long enough and you'll see it. Waves rise, crest, break and pull back, some gentle and some with real force, and each one is different and none of them stays.

If you were the wave you'd think the rising and falling was all there was, and every crest would be permanent and every trough unbearable. But you're not the wave, you're the ocean that holds it.

The waves of your life are so defining from the inside. The anger feels permanent and the shame feels like a verdict. And underneath all of it there's something steadier, the part of you that can notice the wave without being it. That's what I've been building with you for three modules, and in this one it's what does the surfing.

You still feel it, and you still grieve and rage and ache. You just don't get tossed the same way, because something stays steady underneath.

## The Vibrating String

Feelings have a physics to them, and this is the piece I most wish somebody had given me at thirty.

Pluck a guitar string and it vibrates. The sound is loud and clear, then the vibrations slow, the sound softens, and it fades. Nothing was done to it. The string ran its course.

A feeling works the same way. Something happens, a disappointment, a remark from your boss, and it sets off a vibration in your body. The first wave is sharp, and within minutes it would soften on its own, and within hours, mostly, it would be gone.

Unless you keep plucking the string.

Which is what I do, and what you do, without noticing. I rehearse the frustration. I replay the argument in the shower. I compose the email I'd never send. I tell it to a friend who confirms I was right. Every one of those is another pluck, the natural fade gets interrupted, and a feeling that would have run twenty minutes stretches across three days.

Part of what I'm asking of you this module is that you stop plucking. Not by suppressing it, which is its own kind of plucking, but by letting the string play itself out.

## Believe the Feelings, Not the Thoughts

A client of mine found a one-line version of this that she now says to herself when she's caught, and I've been quoting it to people ever since.

*Believe the feelings, not the thoughts.*

What she meant is that when something fires, the body produces a real signal. A tight throat, a racing heart, heat in the chest. I'd trust that.

But the moment the signal lands, the mind arrives with a story. *He doesn't respect me. I'm going to lose my job. They saw through me. I'm too much.* The story comes so fast it feels like perception rather than interpretation. It's usually wrong about the specifics and almost always wrong about the verdict it reaches about you.

So her move, when she's activated, is to stay with the throat and let the thoughts drift past the way you'd let any thought drift past in a sit. The signal is real and the verdict is an overlay. One is information and the other is fiction, and you can tell them apart by where they live.

## Neither Express Nor Repress

My first long meditation teacher was S.N. Goenka, at a Vipassana retreat in Sri Lanka in my twenties. Ten days of silence, sitting for hours, watching sensation arise and pass. The line of his I still use, thirty years on, is four words.

*Neither express nor repress.*

Repress a feeling and you push it underground, and it doesn't leave. It moves into the body and lives in your shoulders, your jaw, your gut, and it comes out sideways as the headache, the insomnia, the inexplicable shortness with your kid.

And express it outward and you scatter it. You yell at the person in front of you who isn't the cause, or you send the email. The charge does drop for a moment, and then it's back, because nothing was metabolized. You discharged it onto somebody else.

The third move is neither. You feel it, all the way, in your own body, without throwing it and without burying it.

I want to say why that line took hold in me, because it wasn't the teaching that did it. I went into that retreat at twenty-something already good at both of the other two. I could bury a thing so well I didn't know it was there, and when it got past me it came out at whoever was nearest. What I hadn't done, once, was feel something through without doing either.

What you learn on a retreat like that, by sheer repetition, is that everything passes. Not as an idea. As a fact you can't argue with. I sat with leg pain at hour six and the pain peaked and went. I sat with rage on day three and the rage peaked and went. Grief ambushed me on day seven and it peaked and went. The body learns impermanence the way a child learns to walk, through reps rather than concepts, and equanimity is what's left over afterward. You don't have to make yourself equanimous. The body knows what it knows.

## The Granite and the Silk Scarf

A client I'll call Claire came in one session with the hardest version of this sitting on her chest.

She was talking about the resignation she carried about her career. A heaviness. A sense that giving her life to the billable hour was inevitable, that the groove she'd worn was cut into granite, and that she'd be paying out of it for the rest of her working life. The feeling was so deep it didn't read as a feeling to her. It read as a fact.

What I gave her that day was an image I first heard from Ram Dass. A student asks how many lifetimes it takes to reach enlightenment, and the Buddha answers with a picture. A crane flies over a granite mountain once every hundred years and brushes a silk scarf across the stone. However long it takes that scarf to wear the mountain down, that's how long.

I don't tell it to discourage anybody. I tell it because what the scarf is doing to the granite is exactly what we're doing to the deepest grooves in your nervous system. The scarf is your attention. The granite is the pattern worn into you over decades. Every time you sit with the feeling instead of pushing through it or getting away from it, the scarf passes over the stone once more.

And for a long time nothing appears to change. The granite is still granite, the silk hasn't left a mark, and the work looks like nothing at all, which is the point where I lose most people. Then, after enough returns, the stone changes shape. Not because the crane forced it. Because the scarf kept coming back, with no demand for results.

Claire's resignation didn't lift in a session with me, or in a year of them. But every time she sat with it rather than pushing through it, the scarf went across the stone once more, and I can tell you what she sounds like now compared to then.

That's what I'm actually asking of you. Not that you stop feeling what's hard, but that you trust the wearing-down, stop demanding the granite turn to sand by Tuesday, and keep showing up with the scarf.

## Summary

- Everything that arises passes. Your body has to feel that enough times to stop arguing with it, because knowing it has never once helped in the moment.
- You're not the wave. You're the ocean that holds it.
- Feelings fade on their own unless you keep plucking the string by rehearsing the story.
- Believe the feelings, not the thoughts. The signal is information and the verdict is an overlay.
- Neither express nor repress. Feel it in your own body without throwing it or burying it.
- The granite gets worn down by patient return, not by force.

## Action

Keep sitting, and before you go on I'd like you to take one sentence of mine and live with it for a day or two.

*Believe the feelings, not the thoughts.*

I'd use a small moment of activation rather than a big one. Notice the body's signal, notice the verdict that lands on top of it, and see if you can keep your attention on the first while the second goes past.
