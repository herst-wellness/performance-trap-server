# The Law of Change

*If you'd prefer to hear this teaching rather than read it, [listen to the audio version](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/s018g18aoukzl7l03bswv/The-Freedom-of-Impermanence.mp3?rlkey=zupkoaavfrhgzd2vtisopvedc&st=23arrc5e&dl=0). The audio covers the same ground as the chapter, with one addition not in the chapter text: the story of my brother's death and how that crisis was the first time I felt impermanence in my bones rather than as a concept.*

Everything changes. It's the most obvious fact in the world, and it's almost impossible to actually live by.

Leaves turn brittle. Seasons pass. Children outgrow their clothes overnight. Bodies age. Relationships shift. Jobs end. Moods come and go like weather. We've seen it a thousand times. And yet, when the next thing we love starts to change, or the next thing we hate refuses to, we react as if it were an exception. We grip what feels good, hoping it might stay. We brace against what hurts, as if bracing could stop it from arriving. We keep losing the same game.

The Buddha called this *anicca*. Impermanence. The constant flux of all things. It's the first of three marks of existence in Buddhist psychology, and it's the one most of the work of equanimity rests on. Because once your body actually knows, in its bones, that everything passes, the equation underneath your reactions changes. The bracing relaxes. The clinging loosens. The bargaining with reality stops. You're left with what's actually here.

## The Wave and the Ocean

Stand on a beach long enough and you'll see the pattern. Waves rise. Waves crest. Waves break. Waves pull back. Some are gentle. Some come in with force. Each one is different. None of them stays.

If you were the wave, you'd think the rising and falling was all there was. You'd think every crest was permanent and every trough was unbearable. But you're not the wave. You're the ocean that holds it. The depth underneath all that movement.

That's what we're trying to remember.

The waves of your life, the emotions, the circumstances, the wins, the losses, can feel so defining when you're inside them. The anger feels permanent. The shame feels like a verdict. The relief feels like solid ground. But underneath all of it, there's something steadier. The part of you that can notice the wave without being the wave. The part that can feel the rising and falling without losing itself in either one. That part is what the practices in Modules 1, 2, and 3 have been building. In Module 4, that part is what does the surfing.

When you identify less with the wave and more with the ocean, you suffer less when life shifts. You still feel it. You still grieve. You still rage. You still ache. But you don't get tossed around the same way, because some part of you stays steady underneath.

## The Vibrating String

Emotions have a physics to them. A guitar string, plucked, vibrates. At first the sound is loud and clear. Then the vibrations slow, the sound softens, and eventually it fades. Nothing did anything. The string just ran its natural course.

A feeling works the same way. Something happens, a disappointment, a surprise, a remark from your boss, and it sets off a vibration in your body. The first wave is sharp. Within minutes, it would naturally soften. Within hours, in most cases, it would fade.

Unless you keep plucking the string.

This is what most of us do without realizing. We rehearse the frustration. We replay the argument in the shower. We compose the email we'd never send. We tell the story to a friend who tells us we're right. We cling to a joyful moment, willing it not to end. Every time we do, we pluck the string again. The natural fade gets interrupted. A feeling that would have lasted twenty minutes stretches into days.

The work of this module is partly to learn to stop plucking. Not by suppressing the feeling, which is its own form of plucking. Just by letting the string play itself out.

## Believe the Feelings, Not the Thoughts

A client of mine, I'll call her Maya, found her way to a one-line version of this teaching that she now repeats to herself when she gets caught.

*Believe the feelings, not the thoughts.*

What she meant was this. When something fires, the body produces a real signal. A tight throat. A racing heart. Heat in the chest. The signal is information. It's worth trusting.

But the moment the signal arrives, the mind shows up with a story. *He doesn't respect me. I'm going to lose my job. They saw through me. I'm too much. I'm not enough.* The story arrives so fast it feels true. It often isn't. The story is the mind's interpretation, filtered through decades of conditioning, of what the body is telling it. The story is usually wrong about specifics, and almost always wrong about the verdict it's reaching about you.

So Maya's move, when she gets activated, is to listen to the body and let the thoughts drift. She'll notice the throat is tight and stay with the tightness. She'll skip the line *he doesn't respect me*. The signal is real. The story is the mind's overlay. One is information. The other is fiction.

This is one of the cleanest shortcuts to equanimity I know. Not a slogan. A practice. The next time something fires, see if you can put your attention on the sensation and let the verdict the mind is forming float past, the way you'd let a thought float past in meditation. Believe the feelings. The thoughts can wait.

## This Too Shall Pass

There's an old story about two brothers who found a small packet after their father died. Inside were two stones. One was a precious diamond. The other was an ordinary rock. The older brother took the diamond for himself and left his sibling the plain stone. But etched into the ordinary stone were four words: *This too shall pass.*

The younger brother kept that rock close. He'd touch it through his pocket during the day. When things were going well, he'd hold it and remind himself: *this too shall pass.* When hardships came, he'd do the same. The reminder didn't take away his joy or his pain. It gave him perspective. He knew the rising and falling would happen no matter what he did. So he didn't cling too hard to spring or despair too deeply in winter.

His brother, who got the diamond, lived at the mercy of his circumstances. He celebrated wildly in good times and fell apart in bad ones. He fought the waves, and the waves thrashed him. The younger brother, by contrast, learned to ride them.

That's equanimity. Not numbness. Not detachment. Just the steady knowledge that everything moves. The capacity to hold joy without clinging and to feel pain without running. Tara Brach calls it *a heart that is ready for anything*. I think that's the cleanest definition I've heard.

## Neither Express Nor Repress

My first long-form meditation teacher was S.N. Goenka, in a Vipassana retreat in Sri Lanka in my twenties. The line of his I keep coming back to, twenty-some years later, is four words.

*Neither express nor repress.*

If you repress an emotion, you push it underground. It doesn't go away. It moves into the body and lives in your shoulders, your jaw, your gut. It comes out sideways. It becomes the headache, the insomnia, the inexplicable shortness with your kid.

If you express the emotion outward, you scatter it. You yell at the person in front of you who isn't really the cause. You write the email you regret. You burn through a relationship to get rid of the charge. The charge does leave for a moment. It comes back, because you didn't actually metabolize it. You just discharged it onto someone else.

The third move is neither. You feel it. All the way. In your own body. Without throwing it and without burying it.

A client of mine, I'll call her Claire, lived inside the trap of those first two for years. Her version of expression was righteous indignation. She'd defend a colleague who'd been wronged with the full force of an Avenger. Her version of repression was the straitjacket she put on the same energy at home, where it wasn't safe to be that big. Both moves left her sour. Neither one moved the actual charge.

The third option for her was to use her own body as the surfboard. The anger would rise. She'd locate the heat, sit with it, let the wave do what it was going to do. The first few times she tried it, she didn't trust the wave would actually pass. It always did. And on the other side, the action she could take was different. Cleaner. Not righteous and not silenced. Just direct.

## The Granite and the Silk Scarf

Claire came in one session with the hardest version of this teaching sitting on her chest.

She was talking about a feeling of resignation she carried about her career. A heaviness. A sense that giving her life to the billable hour was inevitable, that the groove she'd worn was carved into granite and she'd be paying out of that groove for the rest of her working life. She couldn't see a way out. The feeling was so deep it didn't even feel like a feeling. It felt like a fact.

What I sat with her on that day was an image I'd first heard from Ram Dass, drawn from an old Buddhist teaching about lifetimes. The teaching goes like this. A student asks the Buddha how many lifetimes it takes to attain enlightenment. The Buddha answers with an image: imagine a crane that flies, once every hundred years, over a granite mountain. Each pass, the crane brushes a silk scarf across the stone. The number of lifetimes it takes for the scarf to wear the mountain down to nothing: that's how long enlightenment takes.

I don't share that to discourage anyone. I share it because the image of what the scarf is doing to the granite is exactly what we're doing here, in this work, with the deepest grooves in your nervous system. The scarf is your attention. The granite is the pattern that's been worn into you over decades. Each time you sit with the feeling instead of pushing through it or distracting away from it, the scarf passes across the stone one more time.

For a long, long time, nothing seems to change. The granite is still granite. The silk hasn't left a mark. The work looks like nothing.

But eventually, after enough returns, the stone changes shape. Not because the crane forced anything. Not because the scarf was strong. Because the scarf came back, over and over, with patience, with no demand for results.

The granite of the resignation Claire was carrying doesn't go away in a session. It doesn't go away in a year. But every time she sat with it, instead of pushing through it or distracting away from it, the scarf passed across it one more time. That's the practice. That's all of it.

This is what the law of change actually asks of you in practice. Not that you stop feeling what's hard. Just that you trust the wearing-down. That you stop demanding the granite turn to sand by Tuesday. That you keep showing up with the scarf.

## Where These Teachings Come From

I want to name the lineage behind what we just walked through, the way I have in earlier modules. The whole frame of equanimity-as-capacity, equanimity grounded in the direct experience of impermanence, comes most directly from one teacher and one tradition.

My first long-form meditation teacher was **S.N. Goenka**, in a Vipassana retreat in Sri Lanka in my twenties. Goenka was the modern lay teacher who carried the Burmese Vipassana lineage of Sayagyi U Ba Khin into the world, eventually opening centers across India and then globally. The format of his retreats is the same everywhere: ten days of silence, sitting hours a day, watching sensation arise and pass.

What you learn in a Goenka retreat, by direct repetition, is that everything passes. Not as an idea. As a felt, irrefutable fact. You sit with leg pain at hour six of a sitting and the pain peaks and the pain goes. You sit with rage at hour two of day three and the rage peaks and the rage goes. You sit with grief that surprises you on day seven and the grief peaks and the grief goes. Over and over. The body learns *anicca*, impermanence, the way a child learns to walk: through reps, not concepts.

That repetition is what builds equanimity-as-capacity. Goenka used to say, *neither express nor repress*, the line I quoted earlier in this chapter. He also said, simply: *every sensation arises only to pass away*. When that becomes a felt truth instead of an idea, the bracing relaxes on its own. You don't have to force yourself to be equanimous. The body just knows what it knows.

One more teaching from him worth carrying. Goenka often pointed his students past the apparent cause of their suffering. *Outside, outside, outside*, he'd say, naming the habit most of us run, blaming the spouse, the boss, the kid, the situation. The lever isn't there. The lever is inside, in the body's sensations, in the breath. As he put it: *you can certainly change yourself.* Not by trying to change others. By turning your attention to what's actually happening in you.

The Vipassana tradition itself is older. It comes through Theravada Buddhism, which traces its lineage back to the historical Buddha. The word *vipassana* means *clear seeing*: specifically, seeing the three marks of existence (impermanence, the unsatisfactoriness of clinging, and the absence of a fixed self) as they actually show up in the body's moment-to-moment experience. Equanimity is the natural fruit of that seeing, not a quality you cultivate separately.

You don't need to do a Goenka retreat to do this work. You don't need to know any of these names. I'm telling you because the practice has weight behind it. People have been training this capacity for two and a half thousand years. The Equanimous meditation you'll begin this module sits inside that lineage.

If you want to encounter Goenka's teaching directly, the Vipassana Research Institute distributes his discourses freely. Their ten-day silent retreats, held at centers around the world, are also free of charge and are the most direct way to do the practice in the lineage's full form.

## Why This Matters

The thing equanimity actually frees you from isn't your feelings. The feelings keep coming. The freedom is from the loop that gets built on top of them. The grip on what feels good. The bracing against what hurts. The thrashing in the wave. The plucking of the string. All of that adds suffering on top of the feeling itself.

When you stop fighting the rising and falling, you discover something you didn't know was available. A spaciousness underneath the wave. A part of you that can hold what's there without being defined by it.

That's the capacity Module 4 is building. The next chapter, **04.03 Surfing an Emotion**, is the practice that builds it.

## Summary

- The law of change (Buddhist *anicca*, impermanence) is the first foundation of equanimity. Once the body knows everything passes, the bracing relaxes.
- The wave and the ocean. You're not the rising and falling. You're the depth underneath.
- The vibrating string. Feelings have a natural arc. They rise, peak, and fade, unless you keep plucking the string by rehearsing the story.
- *Believe the feelings, not the thoughts.* The body's signal is information. The mind's verdict is interpretation.
- *Neither express nor repress.* The third move is to feel the wave in your own body, all the way through, without throwing it or burying it.
- The granite and the silk scarf. The deepest grooves get reshaped not by force but by patient return. The scarf of your attention, coming back over and over.

## Action

Continue your daily meditation. Before you move on, sit with one sentence for a day or two.

*Believe the feelings, not the thoughts.*

See what changes when you actually try this in a small moment of activation. You don't need a big one. Notice the body's signal. Notice the mind's verdict that arrives on top of it. Practice keeping your attention on the first and letting the second pass.
