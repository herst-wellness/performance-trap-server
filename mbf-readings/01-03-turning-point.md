# Your Turning Point

In Session Preparation I asked you to bring me things you've spent a long time not saying, and I said I wouldn't hide behind expertise. So mine goes first.

Turning points don't always come with fanfare and mine certainly didn't. There was no clean break, no phone call, no diagnosis. The thing that came undone was, of all things, a knot in my gut that wouldn't go away, and the realization, when it finally landed, wasn't about what I needed to do at all. It was about who I'd been pretending to be for forty years.

I was around fifty. (I'm fifty-two now.) When the COVID lockdowns hit, I thought, *Oh shit, my practice is done for.* Everything I'd built had relied on being in the same room with another person. But instead of dissolving, my practice exploded. I started getting calls from all over the world. Japan, Europe, the Middle East, all across the U.S. The work cracked open, and I was coaching leaders through breakdowns they hadn't been able to name until everything stopped.

On the surface, everything looked fine. Underneath, something was beginning to come undone.

## The Tap

I'd close my eyes and start to drift off to sleep, and then I'd feel this tap in the same spot every time, the lower left side of my gut. It didn't feel like pain exactly. More like a message.

*Hello, are you awake? It's our favorite hour. Don't fall asleep. Let's talk.*

Then I'd be jolted back awake, like my body just couldn't let go into the surrender of sleep. That's when the racing thoughts would kick in. What was wrong with me. Why I couldn't sleep. Whether I was going to fall apart on my calls the next day. How much longer I could keep this up before something really broke.

I tried everything I knew. Instead of sleeping, I'd sit upright in a meditative position and not move for two hours. I overhauled my diet. I took more herbal remedies than I care to admit. I did a ridiculous amount of yoga. Nothing made a dent.

Whatever this was, it was in control, not me. And it didn't just feel physical. It felt old. Like something I'd buried a long time ago was pushing its way back up.

It had crept in slowly, starting as a little gut pain, some tightness, a few nights of bad sleep, all of which I'd blamed on an old stomach bug I picked up in India in my early twenties. By fifty the cramping and the bloating and the inability to really eat anything had become a permanent fixture. And now it was wrecking my sleep too.

I told myself I could handle it. I couldn't.

## The Knot

So I called Randall, who I'd worked with years before, back when things were calmer. I'd been resistant to going back to being coached myself. But I'd run out of tools, and what I needed was someone who could sit in it with me without rushing to fix it.

"Where do we start?" Randall asked.

I didn't know. My body felt like it was coming apart. So I said the first honest thing I could. "My gut's a wreck. It cramps all the time. It feels bloated and tight. I'm wiped out. My back hurts. I can't sleep. Everything's just..." I gave a half-laugh. "Honestly? I feel like I'm coming apart. Randall, can you fix me?"

He chuckled. "Alright. Why don't you close your eyes? Take a breath. See what's going on in there."

That was the last thing I wanted to do. But I sat back and closed my eyes and scanned my body, and it didn't take long.

"There's this knot," I said, without opening my eyes. "In my gut. Low down and deep. It just sits there."

He waited.

"It feels stuck, like a rock in there." I opened my eyes, swallowed. "Like something's been sitting in there for years, and it doesn't want to move."

I stayed with it longer than I usually would, not analyzing it, just feeling it. The heat. The density. The way it pulsed, like it had been waiting for me to notice it all along.

At some point, as the feelings began to settle, Randall asked, "If it could talk... what might it say?"

I shifted in the chair. I looked down. "I don't know. Probably something like... 'I'm holding it together.'" I paused. "I've always been holding it together."

Then I felt my throat tighten.

And then it popped out. "Before he took his life, Scott was the problem. I was the proof our family wasn't totally fucked up. I was the good kid."

I exhaled. "His death didn't give me that role. It just made it permanent."

Randall didn't fill the space. He just let me sit in it.

## The Contract

What came out of that session wasn't a strategy, or an insight in any clean sense. It was a contract, an agreement I'd made long before I had words for what I was agreeing to. The terms had been simple. Don't add to the chaos. Be low-maintenance. Hold it together. Be the proof we weren't all fucked up. The contract had been signed by a kid who had no idea he was signing anything. And it had been quietly running my life ever since.

I hadn't chosen any of it. My dad was on the road a lot. When he was home, his moods could turn on a dime. My mom was in over her head trying to raise two boys, one with undiagnosed mental illness and the other pretending he was fine. I figured out fast that I couldn't add to the chaos, so I learned to be low-maintenance. By the time Scott took his life, I was already deep in the role. After, it just became permanent. A few days after we buried him, I was sent back to school with no space to grieve. The message was clear. *You're fine. You got this.* So I was fine. I got it.

And I built a life on it. The competence. The steadiness. The capacity to track tone, match breath, carry someone else's collapse while my own went unnoticed. The thing my clients were paying me for, the very thing they trusted me with, was the same thing that was killing me. I'd built my career on the override. And then COVID hit, and the override stopped working.

## Summary

That was my turning point, and it wasn't a single moment of insight. It was the beginning of a long search into how the rules I'd been living by got laid down before I could read them, and what it was going to take to start living by something else.

What surfaced in Randall's office wasn't unique to me, and the shape of it shows up in almost everyone I work with. A contract signed by someone too young to know they were signing. A role that became the personality. A body keeping the receipts. And what begins to change things isn't the realization itself, it's the willingness, finally, to read what's been written there.

The contract doesn't dissolve when you name it. It just stops being invisible. That's enough to begin.
