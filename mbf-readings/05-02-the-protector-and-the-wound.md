# The Protector and the Wound

*There's [a recording](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/98tdtf7525nr0c0tqv9mj/The-Inner-Critic-Vulnerable-Inner-Child.mp3?rlkey=bbrry38qex174djvfgut0ktrg&st=k4vtj81j&dl=0) in my own voice covering this ground, focused on one common protector and what sits underneath it.*

I said in the Welcome that a protector formed around something, and that what it's been guarding is what I'm going after with you now. So the first question is how you find the thing when the whole point of the protector is that you don't.

And the answer is that you already know where it is, because you've been calling it your problem.

A client I'll call Marco came in convinced his problem was anxiety. And it was, in a way. He had it walking into meetings, and at three in the morning, and in the body buzzing for no reason he could name. He'd done therapy, meditation and a prescription, and none of it touched the thing.

A few months in we found the anxiety wasn't the problem at all. It was the protector. Underneath it, once he could stay long enough to feel the layer below, was something much older and much more tender. *I'm not safe here as myself.* The anxiety was the strategy his system had built so that he'd never fully relax into being himself in a room, and it worked. It kept him vigilant. It also kept him exhausted.

That's the reversal this module runs on, and I think it's the most useful thing in it. **What you've been calling the problem is almost always the protector. The wound is underneath it.**

## The Reversal

You came to me with something specific bothering you, and you named it in About You. Anxiety, like Marco. Or anger, or self-criticism, or the feeling of being chronically behind. A relationship that wasn't working, a body you didn't like, a career that was eating you.

And I'm asking you to consider that the thing you came in with isn't the issue. It's the cover.

I've watched this hold for almost everyone I've worked with. The food project turns out to be sitting on a kid whose emotional life was never registered by the adults around him. The inner critic turns out to be doing the job of keeping a child's anger buried, because if that anger had ever surfaced in that house things would have got dangerous.

Different protectors and different wounds, and the same structure underneath every time. **The protector is whatever you're loudly aware of. The wound is what you're quietly avoiding.**

## How the Protector Formed

Trace them back far enough and they nearly all formed the same way.

A baby comes in needing connection, and I mean the need is biological rather than a preference. The baby reaches, cries, watches the caregiver's face for the cues that tell its nervous system whether the world is safe.

In a perfect setup the caregivers meet that reach with enough consistency that the baby's system learns one thing: *what I bring is welcome here.* That child grows up able to bring themselves without a critic mediating, without an achievement engine running underneath, without a caretaker reflex flipping on whenever somebody in the room is upset.

But almost none of us grew up in that setup. The people raising us were doing their best inside their own conditioning, with their own unmet needs and their own protectors and their own contracts. So the attunement was inconsistent. Sometimes the reach was met and sometimes it wasn't, and sometimes it was met on condition that you adjust the reach to match what they could actually give.

When that happens enough times, something specific gets installed. *The way I naturally am isn't quite right. To stay connected here, I need to adjust.*

And the adjustment becomes the protector.

At the architectural level every protector is the same move. *I will be different from how I am, so that I stay connected, so that I stay safe, so that I don't have to feel what it's like to be unwelcome here.* The shape depends on the room. A critic forms where you had to be hard on your own flaws before anybody else could be. An achiever forms where the room loved performance and was indifferent to your other registers. A caretaker forms where there was a fragile adult whose steadiness you needed to maintain. A suppressor of anger forms where your anger couldn't be tolerated. Same move, different clothes.

That's the contract from Module 1, made flesh. The protector is the contract enforcing itself, in your nervous system, every day.

Mine got installed in a den in Marin County when I was small. My grandfather had his scotch. My grandmother had her bouffant. They were talking about a man who'd admitted he couldn't handle things on his own, and the tone wasn't disappointment. It was disgust. I asked my father what a schnorrer was, and my grandfather put down his glass and told me. A schnorrer is Yiddish for a mooch. There are only two kinds of people, he said, the doers and the schnorrers, and in this family we are doers.

I was a doer for the next thirty years. That's the protector. It built a great deal of my life and it cost me most of what I'm now trying to teach you.

## Honor It Before You Challenge It

Here's where I watch people go wrong.

They get tired of the protector. They name it, they see it as the thing making their life harder, and they set about defeating it.

It doesn't work, and I've never seen it work. The protector is too old, too embedded and too smart, and it's been running the same program for decades. Attacking it just turns the critic on you for failing to get rid of it, and now you've got the original protector plus a fresh layer of failure sitting on top.

What I've seen work is the opposite, which is that you honor it first.

Honoring isn't agreement and it isn't surrender. What I mean by it is acknowledging what the thing accomplished. *You kept me alive in a room where being fully myself would have cost too much. You did your job, you did it well, and you did it for a long time with nobody telling you that you could rest.*

And when the protector hears that, something small shifts. It stops bracing for the attack it expects, and in that softening the work becomes possible.

This is the Ram Dass move I mentioned last module. He used to say of the parts of himself he'd fought with for years: *I invite them in for tea.* Not to convert them and not to argue. To sit with them as guests, with the dignity they earned through long and exhausting service.

I'd try it tonight. When the critic shows up, instead of silencing it or arguing back: *I see you. You've been working hard for a long time. Thank you.* Notice what happens in your body when you offer that. Most people feel a small drop in the bracing. The critic doesn't leave. It softens for a second, and that second is the doorway.

## What It's Protecting You From

This is the language from Internal Family Systems, which I cited in Module 2. Richard Schwartz's principle is clean: every part has positive intent, and there are no bad parts, only parts whose strategies have gone out of date and whose intensity has got out of proportion.

And when I ask a protector what it's actually keeping somebody from, I usually get a clear answer.

The critic is usually protecting you from humiliation. It learned early that naming all your flaws first was safer than being caught with one you hadn't spotted.

The achiever is usually protecting you from invisibility. It learned that being noticed required producing something worth noticing.

The caretaker is usually protecting you from disconnection. It learned that managing the other person's state was how you kept them present enough to reach.

The angry one is usually protecting a younger part that got hurt and was never allowed to say so. The anger is where the pain finally came out, sideways.

The numbing one is usually protecting you from an intensity that really was unbearable once. It learned to take you offline when the wave got too big.

None of them is wrong, exactly. They're out of date, built for a child's nervous system in conditions that have ended, and still running at a child's urgency in an adult's life.

When you understand what a protector has been protecting, two things happen. You stop hating it. And you start being able to see the thing it's been standing in front of.

## You Probably Have More Than One

Most people I work with have a primary that runs most of the time, and a backup or two that take over when the primary can't carry the load.

I worked with a man whose primary was an achiever, built into a career of being indispensable. When achievement wasn't available, in a relationship or in physical pain or in a room where his skills didn't apply, a withdrawer took over and he'd go quiet and become unreachable. And when even that wasn't available, when his partner pushed him to engage, a defensive anger surfaced that made the conversation impossible. Three in a row, all guarding the same thing, which in his case was an old experience of being ordinary and therefore invisible.

Most of us have some version of that. So when you map your own, look for the layers. The first protector you notice is rarely the only one.

## Summary

- What you came in calling the problem is most likely the protector. The wound is underneath.
- It formed early, as an intelligent adaptation to the conditions you were in, and it worked.
- Every protector has positive intent. It's keeping you from something specific: humiliation, invisibility, disconnection, intensity, an emotion that was once dangerous.
- Defeating it doesn't work and adds a layer of failure on top.
- Honoring it first is what creates the small softening the work needs.
- Most of us have a primary and one or two backups underneath it.

## Action

I'd like you to sit with one question for a day or two before you move on.

*What's the protector I notice most often in myself?*
