# The Kids on the Bus

Last chapter you learned to say *there's anxiety here* instead of *I am anxious*. That opens a gap. And the moment there's a gap, a question arrives that couldn't arrive before: if the anxiety isn't me, then what is it?

Here's my answer, and it's the piece of this work people remember longest.

Picture your mind as a bus on a winding road, and you're the driver. Or you're supposed to be. The bus is full of kids, each one a part of you, each with an opinion about where to go and how to get there. Some are quiet. Some are loud and demanding. Some are scared and want to hide, and some are angry and want to take over.

But most of the time the driver isn't really driving. Whichever kid is loudest has the wheel.

That's what I want to work on with you here.

## Why the Metaphor

The metaphor isn't poetic, it's practical. The *I* you experience as one continuous thing is a coalition, and when you say *I'm anxious* you've collapsed something complicated into a single statement. What's truer is that a specific part of you is anxious while other parts are doing other things, and some of them would disagree.

So this is the next step of the same move. *I am anxious* became *there's anxiety here*, and here that becomes *the anxious one has the wheel right now*. Naming the driver gives you something specific to work with rather than a weather system.

## Where the Bus Came From

I got this from my first therapist, a woman named Beverly Peterson, in the early nineties when I was in my twenties. I'd come to her thinking I needed to fix the parts of me that were getting in my way. She gave me the bus and the kids, and what she said to me slowly, over years, was that none of them were getting in my way. They were trying to help. They were stuck.

That reframe changed everything for me, and it's still how I work.

The fuller version of this framework was developed by the psychologist Richard Schwartz in the eighties and nineties. He called it Internal Family Systems, or IFS. If what you read in this chapter resonates and you want to go further on your own at some point, his book *No Bad Parts* is the place I'd send you. The work we'll do here, especially in the next chapter on parts as protectors, is in dialogue with his.

You don't need to know IFS to do this work. I'm naming the lineage so you have somewhere to go if you want it.

## How the Kids Got on the Bus

Most of them got on early.

As a child your nervous system was building strategies for staying connected and safe. The ones that worked became parts. The anxious one who scans the room. The pleaser who reads the air and adjusts. The strong one who says *I'm fine* before anyone asks. The good kid rewarded for not being a problem. Each had a job and each kept doing it long after the circumstances that needed it were gone.

They aren't flaws. They're survival strategies that took on a personality, and they worked, and then they kept working past the point of usefulness.

And that logic doesn't end because you grew up. These parts are still listening. They take over when something now registers as the old danger: a look across a meeting, an email whose tone lands wrong, a silence that doesn't feel right. Any of it can sound to a part of you like the thing it was built to protect you from, and the kid grabs the wheel. It isn't trying to make your life worse. It's doing the job it took on a long time ago.

## When a Kid Grabs the Wheel

You've had moments you can look back on and see you weren't quite yourself. A wave of anxiety took over. You snapped at someone out of all proportion. You bullied yourself so hard the rest of the day was unusable. You couldn't stop working long enough to eat.

None of those are character failures. They're moments when a specific part took the wheel. And what I want from you isn't to stop the kids existing or to quiet them. It's to start seeing which one is driving, while it's driving.

I'll show you three of them from a client I'll call Maya, because I've watched this recognition land in her more clearly than almost anyone.

## The Rabbit

Maya came in one week describing what she called the Rabbit, the part of her always looking around corners and predicting worst cases, generating what she called a nervous foam so she'd never drop the ball or get hurt or miss anything. It was loud and exhausting and she hated it.

The hating was the problem.

I told her about a wave of panic that had hit me out of nowhere as an adult. I traced it back and it landed on being six, on the other side of my father's office door after he'd shut me out, terrified, and that six-year-old hadn't moved much in the forty-odd years since. What finally helped was putting my hand on my belly and saying to him, *I got you. I know you're scared. I got you.*

Maya tried it. *I'm here, Rabbit. I know you're scared.* And almost immediately the Rabbit stopped hopping and just sat, and the energy in her chest softened. It didn't disappear. It calmed down.

What she learned slowly was that the Rabbit didn't need destroying. It needed parenting.

## The Firefighter

The Rabbit's sister was the Firefighter. When the Rabbit sounded the alarm the Firefighter took over, and where the Rabbit was anxious the Firefighter was manic, springing into action and working until the fire was out. Except the fire was never out. There was always another one.

It consumed her. She couldn't enjoy ten minutes walking in Golden Gate Park, because it wanted her back at work. It ran on the belief that resting was selfish, and that she had to keep hoarding achievements to survive, like a kid stuffing uneaten marshmallows in her pockets.

You can't tell a Firefighter to relax. It doesn't believe in relaxation. It believes that if it stops, everything collapses.

So what worked was negotiation, in its own language. Maya learned to talk to it about the ten-year horizon instead of today's list. *We're not stopping. But if we don't rest today we burn out in year three and lose everything, so we're resting to protect the long game.*

It could hear that. It still didn't like resting, but it could agree to it for strategic reasons. Parts don't take commands. They negotiate.

## The Hulk

A different kid surfaced when a colleague of hers was passed over for a promotion she'd earned. Maya watched it happen and felt something in her chest she hadn't felt in years.

She called it the Hulk. Muscles tightening, fists clenching, righteous indignation. It wanted to march into the boss's office and unload.

Her default was to put it in a straitjacket. She values being calm, she fears conflict, and she'd built a self that didn't make scenes, so she stuffed it down. But it didn't go anywhere. It sat in her chest as a tightness for a week and leaked out as bitterness about her workplace, and what the suppression cost her was rumination she couldn't shake.

What we found was a third option between suppression and explosion. She turned toward it and said, *I see how strong you are. I know you're upset. We were disrespected.* She let herself feel the energy without acting on it, and the tightness dispersed.

What she realized sitting with it was that the Hulk never wanted to hit anyone. It wanted acknowledging. It wanted her to know something real had happened and that what she felt made sense, and once it was heard it stopped needing the wheel.

And the deeper one: she'd needed that energy years earlier. Not to explode with. To set what she came to call her little nos, the small refusals that would have kept her from ever reaching boiling point.

## The Inner Critic

Marcus, from the last chapter, is the same thing seen from inside. The critic is one of these kids and usually the loudest, and what makes it the hardest to catch is that it doesn't sound like a kid. It sounds like you. When the Rabbit speaks you can almost feel a different texture in the voice. When the critic speaks, most people just hear themselves, and *I'm a failure* arrives as a verdict from the source rather than a line from a passenger.

It's a passenger that's been driving so long you forgot it was a passenger.

## What Comes Next

The Rabbit and the Firefighter and the Hulk and the critic all turn out to be protectors, each with an intent that made sense once, and meeting them as protectors rather than problems is where this goes next.

For now, just start seeing them. When you're caught in a story or a feeling, notice that a specific kid is driving, and get curious about which.

## Before You Put This Down

Look away and see how many of these you can say back. Getting them wrong is fine and still does the work.

- You're a coalition, not a single self. I call them the kids on the bus.
- They formed as survival strategies and took on a personality. Not flaws.
- They grab the wheel when something now registers as the old danger.
- They don't take commands. They respond to being recognized, parented, or negotiated with in their own language.
- The inner critic is the hardest to catch, because it sounds like you.
