# Further Reading

A parting gift.

These are the books that have shaped the work you've just done. Not all of them. The ones I keep coming back to, the ones I quietly recommend when someone asks. The list is organized by the territory it covers in the program, so you can return to whichever vein of the work has stayed most alive in you.

You don't need to read all of them. You don't need to read any of them. The work is in you now. But if the next stretch of the path calls for a deeper companion, these are the ones I'd put in your hands.

## On the breath and the body

**James Nestor: *Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art***
The book I gave you in Module 1. A journalist who spent years investigating how we breathe, why most of us do it badly, and what changes when we relearn. Approachable, well-researched, and surprisingly moving.

**Bessel van der Kolk: *The Body Keeps the Score***
The foundational text on how trauma lives in the body and how the body, not the mind alone, is the site of healing. Long, sometimes dense, worth the time. If you only read one book from this list, it's a strong candidate.

**Peter Levine: *Waking the Tiger* and *In an Unspoken Voice***
Levine developed Somatic Experiencing, one of the lineages this work draws from. *Waking the Tiger* is the accessible introduction. *In an Unspoken Voice* is the deeper exposition for readers who want the full theory.

## On parts and the inner cast

**Richard Schwartz: *No Bad Parts***
The clearest introduction to Internal Family Systems, which sits behind much of what we did in Module 2 with the kids on the bus and the protectors. Schwartz writes accessibly and his framework gives you a working vocabulary for parts work you can keep using for the rest of your life.

**Hal and Sidra Stone: *Embracing Our Selves***
An earlier and more contemplative companion to Schwartz. The Stones developed Voice Dialogue, which influenced IFS and a lot of subsequent parts work.

## On the sacred wound and what it asks of us

**Pema Chödrön: *When Things Fall Apart***
Pema's most-loved book. Short chapters, deep wisdom, on how to meet difficulty without armoring against it. A good bedside companion for the years ahead.

**Harold Kushner: *When Bad Things Happen to Good People***
The book I quoted in Module 4. Written by a rabbi after losing his son. Honest about what suffering does and doesn't mean, and what we can ask of God or life when the worst arrives. Doesn't try to make it tidy.

**A.H. Almaas: *The Pearl Beyond Price***
For readers ready for a longer immersion. Almaas developed the Diamond Approach, a synthesis of depth psychology and contemplative work. *The Pearl* is dense and rewards slow reading. Almaas is the source for the idea that what feels like a wound is often the protective shell around a quality of essence trying to emerge.

## On the third option and the work of practice

**Werner Erhard: *Speaking Being* (Bruce Hyde and Drew Kopp)***
The closest thing to a primary text on the body of work that gave us the language of the withhold. Hyde and Kopp were students of Erhard who translated his teaching into a more scholarly form.

**Eugene Gendlin: *Focusing***
The classic. Gendlin developed Focusing as a way to listen to the body's felt sense and let it guide what wants to happen next. Short, practical, deeply useful for the work of intuition we did in Module 7.

**Otto Scharmer: *Theory U***
A bigger framework, written for organizations and leaders, but the core move is the same one we've been training. Sense, presence, allow, act. Useful if you're trying to bring this work into your professional life.

## On the wider arc and the hero's journey

**Joseph Campbell: *The Hero with a Thousand Faces***
The original. Dense in places. The book that introduced the monomyth and made Module 8 possible.

**Christopher Vogler: *The Writer's Journey***
Vogler is a Hollywood screenwriter who translated Campbell into a working framework for storytelling. If Campbell feels heavy, start here. The structure is the same. The prose is easier.

**Robert A. Johnson: *He, She, We, Inner Work***
Johnson was a Jungian analyst with a gift for short, accessible books. Any of his are worth your time. *Inner Work* is a clean introduction to working with dreams and active imagination, both of which extend what you've already begun in this program.

## On meditation as a long practice

**S.N. Goenka: *The Discourse Summaries***
My own teacher in the Vipassana tradition. The Discourse Summaries are the talks he gave at his ten-day retreats, transcribed. The clearest version of how the body becomes the door into liberation in the tradition we've drawn from.

**Sharon Salzberg: *Lovingkindness***
The book that brought metta practice into modern Western Buddhism. Direct, warm, accessible. A companion to the Module 8 sit.

**Chögyam Trungpa: *The Myth of Freedom***
For readers ready for a deeper Buddhist text. Trungpa was an unconventional teacher, but his writing on the path is among the most honest in the modern tradition. He's also where the epigraph at the top of Protecting Your Sapling comes from.

## On the larger civilizational moment

**Joanna Macy: *Active Hope***
Macy has spent a lifetime asking how we hold both grief and agency in the face of what's happening to the world. *Active Hope* is the practical companion to that question.

**Iain McGilchrist: *The Master and His Emissary***
A long, demanding book that argues our culture has lost its connection to the right hemisphere's way of knowing, with consequences that ripple through politics, art, work, and our inner lives. Patient readers find it transformative.

## My own book

**Chad Herst: *The Performance Trap***
What I've spent the last several years writing. The book version of the work you and I have done together, available for the next round and for the people in your life you might want to introduce to this work.

## A note on how to use this list

Don't try to read everything. Pick one. Read it slowly. Let it work on you. When that one's done, see what the next thing wants to be. The right book at the right moment is worth ten of the wrong book at the wrong moment.

If you ever want a specific recommendation for what you're working on, send me a note. I'd rather steer you toward the one that fits than have you guess.
