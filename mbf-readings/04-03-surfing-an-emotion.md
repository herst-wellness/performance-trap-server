# Surfing an Emotion

*This is **E** in **SENSE**: equanimity. The capacity to stay through the whole wave without bailing. The fifth move, the close of the inner half.*

Either format works.*

The Law of Change tells you everything passes. Surfing an Emotion is the practice that lets you stay with the thing long enough to actually find out. What we called a sankhara in Module 3 is what we now learn to surf rather than be carried by.

The metaphor of surfing an emotion isn't mine. It comes from the psychologist Raphael Kushner, and what I love about it is that when a surfer is riding a wave, they don't say *that's a good wave* or *that's a bad wave*. The wave they're riding is always the perfect wave. The same is true of the emotional terrain we work with here. We're not busy judging the feeling tone that's arising. We're learning to ride it.

A wave rises in you. You're standing on a surfboard. Your job isn't to stop the wave. Your job is to stay on the board. The wave does what it's going to do, the rising, the peaking, the falling. You stay upright. You ride it to shore.

The surfboard is your awareness, the capacity you built in Module 2 and deepened in Module 3. The wave is the emotion underneath the story. The shore is where the wave finally lays you down, in a different state than it picked you up.

One thing I want to name directly. The wave you're surfing isn't generic emotion. It's a **protector in its physical form.** The throat that clamps. The chest that hardens into a shield. The gut that locks. The shoulders that brace. Each of these is a piece of the older system, doing its old job, working from inside the body to keep you safe. What the protector is protecting you from is the ache underneath, the imprint of every time you contorted yourself to belong. The Still Face the room turned on you. The double bind that didn't let you name it. The override you ran to stay close to the person you needed.

So when you surf, you're not just letting an emotion pass. You're keeping company with a protector that's been doing its job for decades, while the wave it's been holding moves all the way through. That's a more accurate frame than *riding an emotion*. Hold it as you read the rest of this chapter.

## Energy in Motion

The word *emotion* comes from a Latin root, *emovere*, which means *to move out*. Emotions are literally energy in motion. When the energy is allowed to move, it goes through its natural cycle. It rises, it crests, it falls. When the energy is blocked, by suppression, by distraction, by the story we layer on top, it gets stuck. The stuckness is the source of most of what we call our chronic difficulties. The headaches. The insomnia. The chronic shortness with the people we love. The energy was never the problem. The blocking of the energy was.

Surfing an emotion is not making the emotion happen. The emotion is already happening. It's not making the emotion go away. The emotion is going to do what emotions do whether you cooperate or not. Surfing is the act of staying out of the energy's way long enough that it can complete its movement.

## What Makes This So Hard

Most of us, when an emotion arrives, do one of three things.

We resist. We brace against the feeling. We tighten the jaw, hold the breath, clamp the gut. The resistance is supposed to make the feeling smaller. It doesn't. It just keeps the feeling stuck right where it is, pressing against whatever it can't get past.

We distract. We pick up the phone. We pour the drink. We open the spreadsheet. We text the friend. The distraction is supposed to give us a break. It does, for about three minutes. The feeling is still there when we come back, often louder, because nothing got metabolized.

We discharge. We throw the feeling at someone. We blame. We pick a fight. We send the email. The discharge does relieve the pressure briefly. It also damages relationships, costs us self-respect, and rarely changes the underlying charge. By tomorrow we're back where we started, plus an apology to make.

Resistance, distraction, discharge. Three doors. None of them lead to the other side.

Surfing is the fourth door. You stay. You don't brace. You don't distract. You don't discharge. You let the wave do what waves do.

## The Window of Presence

Here's what makes surfing possible. You have a window. It's the range of intensity you can stay with before the system flips into fight-or-flight and you bail off the board.

The window is narrower than you'd hope. Most people, before they've trained this, can stay with discomfort for a minute or two before the part of them that wants out gets too loud to ignore. The part says *get out of here*. It says *fix this*. It says *I cannot feel this one more second*. When the voice gets loud enough, you reach for the phone, or the drink, or the defensive sentence, and the wave dumps you off the board.

The way the window widens isn't by force. You don't grit your teeth and tough it out. That's just a sophisticated form of bracing. The way the window widens is by gentle, repeated practice of staying inside it for a little longer than feels comfortable, then backing off when you need to, then coming back. The titration rhythm from Module 3 lives here too. Touch the sensation, let go, come back. Each time you do, the window opens a bit further.

A client of mine, I'll call him Andre, came into Module 4 with a window so narrow that anything bigger than mild irritation tipped him into shutdown. He had a habit of intellectualizing his way out of every feeling before it could really land. The practice for him wasn't to flood himself with intensity. It was to find a smaller wave he could actually surf, and to sip the sensations rather than gulp them.

He'd start with a moment that brought up only mild discomfort. He'd locate the sensation in his chest. He'd stay for ten seconds. He'd come back to his breath. He'd return for another ten seconds. Then twenty. Then thirty. He was building the window the same way you build muscle. Not by lifting the heaviest weight you can find. By coming back to the gym.

After a few months of this, the bigger waves, the shame and despair that came up when he thought about his relationship with someone he loved, started to be surfable too. Not pleasant. Not comfortable. But surfable. He could stay with the wave instead of disappearing into his head.

## Allowing, Not Solving

The biggest move in surfing, the one that most people get wrong for the longest, is the difference between allowing and solving.

A client of mine, I'll call him Adam, ran into this directly. Adam is a fixer. He's spent his whole life trying to solve every difficulty he's encountered, including his own emotional life. He'd been to therapy. He'd tried medication. He'd tried diet changes, sleep hygiene, supplements. He'd built complex systems to manage his anxiety. None of it touched the actual ache underneath.

One session, after he'd settled enough to notice what was in his body, he found a heavy pit in his stomach. He stayed with it for a minute, started to relax into it, and then, without realizing he was doing it, the part of him that fixes things kicked in. *Okay. How do I solve this?* Within seconds, his body tensed back up.

He noticed it, and he looked confused. He said, *the second I tried to solve it, it tightened again.*

I said back to him, *it doesn't want to be solved. It's asking to be allowed.*

That sentence has become a landmark in our work together. The ache he'd been trying to fix for two decades wasn't asking to be fixed. It was asking to be felt. The fixing was the override in disguise. The fixing was the contract still trying to do its job. The actual move was to do nothing. To let the ache be there. To not look for a way out.

This is harder than it sounds, especially for people whose whole identity has been built around being effective. The capacity to do nothing in the face of something difficult is not a default skill. It has to be trained.

What you'll notice, as you train it, is that the moments you most want to solve something are the moments allowing has the most to offer. The fixing impulse is the protector. It's protecting you from feeling what's underneath. When you can sit with the fixing impulse, without acting on it, the impulse itself starts to soften. And underneath it, the wave you were actually feeling has more room to move.

## Insight on the Other Side

Here's the part most people don't believe until they've felt it. If you stay through the whole wave, what shows up on the other side is something you couldn't have gotten to by thinking.

It might be a memory. It might be a sentence. It might be a clear sense of what the actual next move is. The book name for it is insight. The Buddhist name is *vipassana*. What it actually feels like is *oh. That.*

A client of mine, I'll call him David, came in one session knotted up about a relationship that had been unraveling in slow motion for months. He'd been trying to figure it out for weeks. The figuring was getting him nowhere. We slowed his breath. I had him drop into his chest, where the knot lived. We stayed with it for a few minutes. The knot moved. It became a tightness in his throat. We stayed with that. It moved again. It became a pressure behind his eyes. We stayed with that.

After about ten minutes of riding the changing sensation, his face shifted. He said quietly, *I haven't been honest with him about what I actually want.* That was it. That was the whole thing. He'd been trying to think his way to the next move for weeks. The body knew the next move all along. It just needed him to stay long enough for it to surface.

The action David took after that session was different in quality from anything he could have planned in the middle of the wave. Cleaner. More direct. He calls what shows up on the other side *skillful action*. It's the move you couldn't have reached for in the middle of the rage or the panic or the despair, because in the middle of those, your options were limited to what the reaction would let you do. After the wave passes, a wider set of options opens up. The one you choose from that wider set tends to be the one you can live with afterward.

This is why I keep telling clients not to act while the wave is at its peak. The wave at its peak is the contract speaking. The wave on the other side is you.

## Body Before Story

One more thing that makes surfing possible. You have to leave the story.

When something fires, the mind shows up immediately with an explanation. He doesn't respect me. I'm going to lose this. They saw through me. The story is so fast you don't catch it as a story. It feels like the truth.

If you ride the story, you're not surfing the emotion. You're riding the rumination. The rumination is just plucking the guitar string again. You'll be on it for hours.

The move is to leave the story and go to the body. Where in your body is this living right now? What does it feel like? Tight, hot, heavy, hollow, sharp? You stay with the *what* and the *where*, not the *why*. The why can come back later, often as an insight when the wave passes. While the wave is moving, your job is the sensation, not the meaning.

Adam put it like this once. *I don't care about the story. I just want the sensation.* That was after he'd been trying to tell me a long narrative about something that had happened at Esalen. I stopped him. The story wasn't going to surf him. The pit in his stomach would.

This isn't anti-thinking. It's just sequencing. Sensation first. Insight after. The mind will come back online when it has something useful to add. While the wave is moving, the mind's job is to stay out of the way.

## Connecting to Module 2 and Module 3

Three moves you already know are inside the Surfing practice.

In Module 2, you learned to **meet a part**. The part that's running the show during a hard moment, the kid that grabbed the wheel. Meeting that part is one entry point.

In Module 3, you learned to **Be With** the body's signal before the loop fires. That signal, when you can stay with it, is the wave you're surfing.

In Module 4, you're learning to stay with that signal **through the whole wave**, not just at the front edge. Same body, same signal, same nervous system. The capacity is just being asked to stretch further.

When you sit down to do the surfing practice this week, you'll often find a part of you shows up alongside the wave. The kid. The protector. The one that's still scared. That's fine. The skills layer. Meeting the part and surfing the wave the part is riding are the same move at different scales. You can do both. Sometimes you'll say to the kid, *I see you. I'm here.* Sometimes you'll just stay with the wave. Whatever lets you stay on the board.

## What's Actually on the Other Side

I want to name what we're moving toward, because the practice is easier when you know the destination.

What's revealed when a protector settles, and the wave it's been guarding moves through, is the self you came in with, before you learned to contort to belong. Most of us, by the time we're adults, are living as a collection of roles. The polished one. The performer who says the right things. The achiever who proves he deserved the seat at the table. The one who fits what others need. The one who hides the pain. We didn't choose any of these consciously. They got handed to us, decade by decade, every time the room asked us for something specific and we delivered. Eventually the collection of roles started to feel like the whole of who we are.

It isn't. Underneath the roles, there's still the you that existed before any of them got assigned. That you has been quietly hosting all the performances while still being something underneath them. That's what gets revealed when the protector settles. Not a hidden essence. Not a soul. Just the part of you that was already there before the contortion started, the part the performance has been speaking on behalf of.

To be clear: I'm not pointing at anything mystical. I'm not handing you a spiritual concept. I'm pointing at something verifiable. You know when you've spent a weekend with someone you can fully be yourself around, and you walk away lighter, looser, more recognizable to yourself? That lightness isn't a different self. It's the self that was always there, briefly given room. What the surfing practice does, over time, is give that self room more often.

The surfing practice is how you come back. Each wave you ride to shore is one more pass of the silk scarf across the granite of the contortion. Each pass, the protector relaxes a little further. Each relaxation, a little more of you has room. This is the homecoming Module 4 is building toward.

I'm naming this explicitly because I want you to understand the stakes. The point of staying with the wave isn't just to feel better afterward, though you usually do. The point is to come back to the parts of yourself you left behind to survive. Equanimity is the capacity that makes that return possible. Without enough capacity to feel the contortion as it loosens, the protector won't let go. With enough capacity, the protector finally trusts that you can hold what's underneath, and the parts you left behind have room to come back.

This is also why this module is the hinge into STEP. You can't renegotiate the contract you signed as a child until you've come back to the one who signed it. SENSE brings you home. STEP, in Modules 6 and 7, is what becomes possible once you're home. You start to see the trades you've been making, the contracts you've been honoring, and you discover, for the first time, that you have standing to renegotiate them. The renegotiation can't happen until you've actually come back. That's the work this module sets up.

The clearest one-line version of what Equanimity actually does: it's receiving a piece of unfair feedback and feeling the defensive rage spike in your chest, but choosing to feel the burn of the anger rather than snapping back or shutting down. The snap is the contract speaking. The shutdown is the contract speaking. The choice to feel the burn instead is the moment STEP becomes possible. That's where the next module's work starts.

## Two Questions for the Body

The two questions I dropped into the body in Heather's session are worth naming as a standalone practice. You can use them any time you've made contact with a sensation and you want to invite it to show you what it's holding.

*What are you trying to prevent?*

*What are you worried might happen?*

These aren't questions you ask with your mind. You drop them into the body and wait. The body responds in its own time. Sometimes with a memory. Sometimes with a sentence. Sometimes with the sensation getting tighter before it loosens. Whatever shows up, you come back to the body and notice what happens next.

These aren't questions asking the protector to explain itself. They're questions that give the protector permission to show you what it's holding. The answer often isn't verbal at all. It's somatic. The clamp shifts. The shield softens. The stone changes location. The shift is the answer.

You'll hear these same two questions in the Equanimous meditation. They're worth carrying with you outside the formal sit.

## What Matters Is the Return

One more thing before we go to *What Changes*, because I want to say it before any of you decide you're failing the practice.

Most people will sit down with the Equanimous meditation and notice, within a minute or two, that they've already drifted. The mind wandered into the email they have to write or the conversation they had this morning. They'll think, *I'm bad at this. I can't even stay for two minutes.*

What matters isn't doing it right. What matters is the return. Over and over, we notice where we leave, and we come back. The leaving isn't the failure. The leaving is the practice noticing itself. The return is the practice. Every time you notice you've drifted and come back to the sensation, that *is* the practice. Not what happens before or after. The return itself.

You're not building the capacity to stay perfectly. You're building the capacity to return. Those are different. The first is impossible. The second is what equanimity actually is.

## What Changes

Over time, with enough practice, the things that used to send you into a spiral start to feel different. Not because the feelings have gone away. They haven't. The fights with your partner still fire something. The slights at work still land. The grief still moves through you on the days when it comes.

What changes is the relationship.

You stop fearing the wave, because you've ridden enough waves to know they pass. You stop bailing at the peak, because you've felt enough peaks that they don't seem as final as they used to. You stop reaching for the phone or the drink or the defensive sentence at the moment of activation, because the part of you that used to need the discharge has had enough chances to discover it didn't actually need it.

The granite of your reactivity wears down. The scarf of your attention has come back enough times.

This is not enlightenment. This is just a quieter system. A wider window. A body that has learned, in small reps over months and years, that it can hold what it once couldn't.

## Summary

- The wave you're surfing is a protector in its physical form. The throat clamp, the chest shield, the locked gut. Each one is doing its old job of guarding the ache underneath.
- Emotions are energy in motion. When the energy is allowed to move, it completes its arc and passes. When it's blocked (resistance, distraction, discharge), it stays stuck.
- The window of presence is the range of intensity you can stay with before flipping into fight-or-flight. The window widens through repeated, gentle practice. Sip the sensation, don't gulp it.
- Allowing, not solving. The fixing impulse is often the protector in disguise. The deeper move is to do nothing and let the wave move.
- Insight emerges on the other side. The clearest next moves usually arrive after the wave passes, not in the middle of it. This is what *skillful action* actually means.
- Stay with body and sensation, not with the story. The story is the rumination. The sensation is the wave.
- What's revealed when the protector settles is the self you came in with, before you learned to contort to belong. The parts you left behind to survive. Coming home means returning to those parts.
- Equanimity is the capacity that makes the homecoming possible. It's also what sets up the work of STEP in Modules 6 and 7. You can't renegotiate a contract until you've come back to the one who signed it.
