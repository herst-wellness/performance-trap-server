# Practice

*This is **P** in **STEP**: practice. The fourth move, and STEP is now whole.*

Expand Options was about widening the field until you can see past the pair. This is what you do once you've seen it, and it's the half that actually changes anybody.

The Welcome already told you why seeing isn't enough, so I won't relitigate it. What matters here is the consequence. If understanding won't update the contract, then something has to be done rather than understood, and this is that.

## What Practice Is

Practice is the moment the old contract gets interrupted in real life, and that's all it is.

Your contract may have said *don't say what you feel, don't need anything, don't disappoint anyone, don't rest, don't push back, keep the peace, stay useful, stay impressive, stay ahead of danger.* Practice is one small thing that breaks the agreement.

And it doesn't have to be dramatic. In my experience it's better when it's small, because a short truthful sentence does more than a confrontation, and a single pause before answering a demand starts to undo a lifetime of urgency.

I'm not asking you to perform courage. I'm asking you to give your nervous system an experience that contradicts the old prediction enough that it has to update.

## The Withhold

Werner Erhard, who built a body of work around personal transformation in the seventies, used a term I've found useful. The withhold.

A withhold is a truth you aren't saying. It can be small, like *I didn't like that joke*, or large, like *I haven't been happy in this marriage for two years*. The size isn't the point. The point is that you're carrying something you haven't put down.

And carrying it costs you, not metaphorically. Your body burns energy managing the unsaid, tracking the room, calibrating tone, editing the next sentence so it stays inside the boundary the withhold requires. Most people I meet don't notice they're doing it because they've always done it, and the fatigue just feels like life.

When you finally say the thing, something unclenches. The energy that went into managing and timing and editing comes back. The other person may not react well, and that isn't the point. The point is that your system stops doing the double-bind arithmetic, and stops feeling one thing while saying another.

## One Conversation, All the Way Through

A client I'll call Lisa had been carrying her boss's stress for years. Not because anybody asked her to, but because she was good at it. She could anticipate his moods, translate his intensity, absorb the pressure, keep the team moving, and still make him feel like the visionary rather than the problem.

The breaking point came in a text. She was at her daughter's basketball game, the first time in weeks she'd felt able to be present with her. He messaged in the middle of it. *Your leadership is a two out of ten right now.* She spent the rest of the game trying to look like she was there.

But the text wasn't the wound. The pattern was. So she gave herself a deadline for the conversation, and on the Friday morning she almost backed out of it.

Not dramatically, because her avoidance always showed up as reasonableness. *Maybe today isn't the right day. Maybe he's under too much pressure. Maybe I should wait until I'm more grounded. Maybe I should say it differently.* That's how the old pattern protects itself. It doesn't say abandon yourself. It says be thoughtful, be fair, don't make it worse.

Before the call she sat with both feet on the floor, and she didn't try to get calm, because that would have been another performance. She let herself feel the shakiness in her chest and the buzzing in her arms, and reminded herself of the move. *I don't have to attack. I don't have to disappear.*

He came on looking tired. *I've only got twenty minutes. What's up?*

Normally that would have been enough to make her edit herself, and she could feel her body starting the calculation. Twenty minutes isn't enough, he's already rushed, bring it up later. But she noticed the calculation, and the noticing was the practice.

*There's something I need to talk about before we get into logistics.*

Then she said it. That the text landed hard. That she knew he was trying to provoke something and wasn't refusing the feedback, but needed him to understand the impact. That she'd been at the game with her daughter and had spent the rest of it gone.

And then the harder one. *I've been carrying your stress for a long time. I don't think I realized how much. And I can't keep doing it.*

Not polished, not wrapped in enough cushioning to guarantee a good response. Just true.

He looked away from the camera and she felt the old panic, and she wanted to rescue him from it. *I know you didn't mean it. I know you're under a lot. Maybe I'm overreacting.* All of it was right there and available.

She didn't say it. She let the silence stand, and that was the practice too.

Eventually he said he hadn't realized it hit her like that. She told him she wasn't making him the villain, but that she needed to stop acting like impact only counts when someone intended it, because that's where she disappears. He said he'd been trying to get her to see her power. She said that when she's already depleted, being hit that way doesn't call her into her power, it knocks her out of herself.

That sentence surprised her. She hadn't written it down.

He didn't fully apologize and he didn't become a different man. But he said he thought he'd relied on her to do that, and she said yes, and that she'd let it happen because it had made her feel valuable. Like if she could carry it, she mattered.

Afterward she didn't feel triumphant, which surprised her too. She felt tired. A different tired from the exhaustion of self-betrayal. The kind that comes after using a muscle that's been neglected for years.

She hadn't attacked him. She hadn't abandoned herself. She hadn't solved the relationship or got perfect reassurance. She'd broken the contract.

## Designing the Alliance

There's a phrase I picked up at the Coaches Training Institute that's stayed with me. Design the alliance.

Once you've said the true thing, the next move is a small agreement about how the relationship will handle it going forward, because the truth isn't the end of the conversation. It's the start of a different arrangement.

Lisa did it without naming it. After *I can't keep doing that*, she said what she needed, which was feedback with more context and better timing, and that she could take hard feedback but couldn't be the container for intensity that hadn't been sorted through first.

Most of us were never taught that second move. We learned either to swallow the truth and stay in the old arrangement, or to deliver it and leave. The third option is to say it and then negotiate the terms. Sometimes the other person won't come in, and that's information, but you don't have it until you've asked.

## Mine, and It's Recent

I wrote a book against the contract that says I have to push past my limits to earn my place. Then hours after finishing the draft I was on a call about how to sell it, and the whole thing became logistics. Cameras, sessions, editing, cost. And I kept saying yes while my whole body was bracing.

And there was the old pair, right on time. Option A, the doer's: say yes to all of it and override my body to make it happen. Option B, the schnorrer's: put it quietly on Amazon, act like I don't need anything, play it cool.

I woke at three in the morning with my gut in knots, and in a session the next day my acupuncturist stopped me from answering the question at all and asked me to describe the geography of the feeling instead. It started at the solar plexus and climbed into my floating ribs, and when I stayed with it long enough the image changed. It wasn't a tunnel. It was armor. A shield across my chest, going down into my quads and the tops of my feet. The same armor I built as a kid.

Twenty years of this work and I still had to be stopped and asked where it lived.

## What the Body Learns

The first time you break the pattern it rarely feels graceful. It feels awkward and exposed, and you may well spend the next day wondering whether you said too much or not enough.

Something learns anyway. *I can survive telling the truth. I can let someone be disappointed without rushing to fix it. I can stay connected to myself while in contact with you.*

You don't become invulnerable and you don't stop caring what people think. You stop paying for that caring with your own disappearance.

## Repetition

Nobody gets this in one clean shot, and I haven't met anyone who did. You'll miss it, overexplain, go quiet, say yes when you mean no, and then you'll notice and try again, and that's the discipline.

There's no version without backsliding and no version where you finish. The contract will keep showing up in new forms for the rest of your life, and the work isn't to never meet it again. It's to recognize it sooner.

The first cycle takes the longest. The fifth is faster. Eventually you catch yourself mid-trade and pull back before the move completes, and that's the actual goal. Not silencing the protector. Shortening the loop.

## Summary

- Practice is the moment the old contract gets interrupted in real life, and small beats dramatic.
- A withhold costs you energy every day you carry it. Saying it gets the energy back.
- After the truth comes the alliance. Say what you need going forward.
- The body learns by surviving it, not by understanding it.
- The loop shortens. Eventually you catch the trade mid-motion.
