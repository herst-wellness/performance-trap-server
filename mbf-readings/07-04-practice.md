# Practice

*This is **P** in **STEP**: practice. The fourth and final move. STEP is now whole.*

The third option appears in the mind. Practice is the moment it enters the body and the world.

This is the second half of what Module 7 is teaching. The first half (Expand Options) is about widening the field so you can see beyond the binary. This half is about what you do once you've seen it.

It would be easier if the seeing was enough. It isn't. The old contract has been written into the body for years, sometimes decades. The body's response to a familiar binary is faster than thought. You can know exactly what the third option is, see it clearly, and still find yourself, an hour later, having done the old thing. Knowing isn't the same as doing.

**P. Practice** is the discipline of doing.

## What Practice Is

Practice is the moment the old contract is interrupted in real life.

The old contract may have said: *don't say what you feel. Don't need anything. Don't disappoint anyone. Don't rest. Don't reveal the vulnerable part. Don't push back. Don't risk rejection. Don't let anyone see the full truth. Keep the peace. Stay useful. Stay impressive. Stay hidden. Stay ahead of danger.*

Practice is the moment you do one small thing that breaks that agreement.

It doesn't have to be dramatic. In fact, it's often better when it's small. A small truthful sentence can be more transformative than a dramatic confrontation. A single pause before responding to a demand can begin to change a lifetime of urgency. A quiet boundary can be more powerful than an explosion. A simple acknowledgment of loneliness can be more healing than another attempt to solve life from the neck up.

The point isn't to perform courage. The point is to give the nervous system a new experience. One that contradicts the old contract enough that the body has to update its prediction.

## The Withhold

Werner Erhard, who developed a body of work in the seventies and eighties around personal transformation, used a term I've found useful: *the withhold.*

A withhold is a truth you're not saying. A feeling you're not naming. A reality you've been quietly managing around. The withhold can be small. *I didn't like that joke.* It can be large. *I haven't been happy in this marriage for two years.* The size isn't the point. The point is that you're carrying something you haven't put down.

The thing about a withhold is that it costs you to keep carrying it. Not metaphorically. Actually. The body burns energy managing the unsaid. Tracking the room. Calibrating tone. Editing the next sentence so it stays inside the boundary the withhold requires. Most of us don't notice we're doing this, because we've been doing it forever. The fatigue feels like *just life.*

When you finally say the thing, something unclenches. The energy that was spent managing, softening, timing, and editing comes back online. You stop organizing your life around what can't be said. The other person may not react perfectly. That's not the point. The point is that your system stops doing the double-bind math. You stop feeling one thing in your body and saying another thing with your mouth. The bracing eases because you're no longer running two tracks at once.

That's the felt physics of the practice. Honest sentence in, energy back, less to manage, more to live with.

## Lisa

A few months ago, I was working with a client I'll call Lisa. She had been carrying her boss's stress for years. Not because anyone explicitly asked her to. Because she was good at it. She could anticipate Jonathan's moods, translate his intensity, absorb the pressure, smooth the edges, keep the team moving, and still somehow find a way to make him feel like the visionary rather than the problem.

That was part of what made them powerful together. She understood him. She could metabolize what other people could not. She could stand close to the heat.

But lately the heat was burning through her.

The breaking point arrived through a text. She was at her daughter's basketball game, the first time in weeks she'd felt she could actually be present with Sienna. Jonathan messaged her in the middle of it. *Your leadership is a two out of ten right now.* She froze. She spent the rest of the game trying to look like she was there, but she wasn't. She was gone.

In our session afterward, we named the pattern. The text wasn't the wound. The pattern was. She had been absorbing Jonathan's intensity for years and metabolizing it as her own depletion. The withhold was: *I can't keep doing that.*

She gave herself a deadline to have the conversation with him. Friday.

Friday morning, she almost backed out.

Not dramatically. That wasn't her style. Her avoidance was quieter than that. It showed up as reasonableness.

*Maybe today isn't the right day.*

*Maybe he's under too much pressure.*

*Maybe I should wait until I'm more grounded.*

*Maybe I should say it differently.*

*Maybe I should take more responsibility first.*

This was how the old pattern protected itself. It didn't say *abandon yourself.* It said *be thoughtful.* It said *be fair.* It said *don't make things worse.* It dressed itself up as maturity.

Underneath all the carefulness, Lisa could feel the same familiar pull. The pressure to soften the truth before it had even left her mouth. The pressure to make sure Jonathan would be okay. The pressure to make sure he didn't feel blamed, criticized, abandoned, or like she was no longer on his side.

His experience had become the center of the conversation before the conversation had even begun.

She opened the notes app on her phone and looked at the sentence we'd worked with.

*I've been picking up on your stress without even realizing it. But I can't keep doing that anymore.*

Her body tightened as she read it. It still felt true. It also felt dangerous.

Before the call, she sat at her desk with both feet on the floor. She didn't try to make herself calm. That would have been another performance. Instead, she let herself feel the shakiness in her chest, the hollow space behind her eyes, the slight buzzing in her arms. Then she put one hand on her belly and reminded herself of the practice.

*I don't have to attack. I don't have to disappear. I can tell the truth and stay in contact.*

When Jonathan appeared on the screen, he looked tired. Distracted. Already moving fast.

*Hey,* he said. *I only have about twenty minutes. What's up?*

Normally that would have been enough to make Lisa edit herself. She could feel the opening closing. Her body immediately began calculating. Twenty minutes wasn't enough time. He was already rushed. This was going to inconvenience him. Maybe she should just cover the agenda items and bring it up later.

She noticed the calculation. That was the practice.

*Actually,* she said, *there's something I need to talk about before we get into logistics.*

Jonathan leaned back slightly. *Okay.*

She could hear the caution in his voice. Or maybe she imagined it. Either way, her system registered danger.

She looked down at her notes, then back up.

*When you texted me at the game and said my leadership was a two out of ten, it really landed hard.*

He started to respond quickly. *Yeah, I was trying to,*

*I know,* she said, gently but firmly. *I know you were trying to provoke something. I know you were trying to challenge me. And I'm not saying there isn't feedback for me to take in. But I need you to understand the impact.*

That sentence mattered. She wasn't refusing accountability. She was refusing to be crushed by the form it came in.

Jonathan was quiet.

She continued.

*I was with Sienna. It was the first time in weeks I felt like I could actually be present with her. And when I saw that message, I froze. I spent the rest of the game trying to look like I was there, but I wasn't. I was gone.*

As she said it, Lisa could feel the old shame rising. The voice that said, *why are you making this personal? Why can't you just handle feedback? Why are you being so sensitive?*

She kept going.

*And I need to say something that's hard for me to say. I think I've been carrying your stress for a long time. I don't think I even realized how much I was doing it. I've been absorbing it, translating it, trying to stay steady enough for both of us. And I can't keep doing that anymore.*

There it was.

Not polished. Not perfect. Not wrapped in enough cushioning to guarantee a good response. Just true.

Jonathan looked away from the camera. For a second, Lisa felt the old panic. She wanted to rescue him from the discomfort. She wanted to say, *I know you didn't mean it. I know you're under a lot. I know I have things to work on too. I'm sorry, maybe I'm overreacting.*

All of that was right there, waiting.

She didn't say it.

She let the silence stand.

That was the practice too.

Finally, Jonathan said, *I didn't realize it hit you like that.*

*I know,* Lisa said. *And I'm not trying to make you the villain. But I need to stop acting like impact only counts if someone intended it. Because that's where I disappear. I tell myself you didn't mean it, and then I make myself responsible for metabolizing the whole thing.*

He nodded slowly.

*I'm trying to get you to see your power,* he said. *That's what I was doing. I don't want you hiding behind being overwhelmed.*

*I hear that,* she said. *And there may be truth in that. But when I'm already depleted, being hit that way doesn't call me into my power. It knocks me out of myself.*

That sentence surprised her. She hadn't written it down. It came from somewhere deeper than preparation.

*It knocks me out of myself.*

Jonathan's face softened.

*Okay,* he said. *That's helpful.*

Lisa felt the smallest wave of relief, but she didn't rush to close the loop. That was another old move: the moment there was even a hint of repair, she would take care of the other person, reassure them, and move on.

Instead, she stayed with herself.

*What I need going forward,* she said, *is for feedback to come with more context and more awareness of timing. I can take hard feedback. I really can. But I can't be the container for intensity that hasn't been sorted through first.*

Jonathan exhaled. *That's fair.*

Then he added, *I think I've relied on you to do that.*

Lisa felt something in her chest loosen.

He hadn't fully apologized. He hadn't suddenly become a different person. The whole relationship had not been magically repaired. But something had shifted. A piece of the invisible arrangement had been made visible.

*Yes,* she said. *I think you have. And I think I let that happen because it made me feel valuable. Like if I could carry it, then I mattered.*

Her eyes filled when she said that. Not because she was falling apart. Because she could feel the truth of it. The little girl who learned to become indispensable. The adult woman who became brilliant at holding impossible things. The leader who confused being needed with being loved.

*I don't want that to be the basis of how we work together anymore.*

He nodded. *I don't either.*

After the call ended, Lisa didn't feel triumphant. That surprised her. Some part of her had imagined that if she finally told the truth, she'd feel powerful and clean and liberated. Instead, she felt tired. But it was a different kind of tired. Not the exhaustion of self-betrayal. Not the depletion of having swallowed one more thing so everyone else could keep moving. This was the tiredness that comes after effort. After doing something new. After using a muscle that had been neglected for years.

She stood up from her desk and walked outside. The air felt cool on her face. For a few minutes, she didn't check her phone. She didn't replay the conversation to find all the places she could have said it better. She didn't immediately turn it into a leadership lesson or a relational strategy.

She just let herself feel what had happened.

She had not attacked him.

She had not abandoned herself.

She had not solved the whole relationship.

She had not gotten perfect reassurance.

But she had broken the contract.

The old contract said: *carry the weight, make it elegant, call it strength, and don't let anyone see what it costs you.*

This time, she had done something else. She had handed back what was not hers. She had stayed in her body while another person had a reaction. She had told the truth without making it a weapon. She had allowed the relationship to encounter her, not just the competent role she usually performed.

That's why the conversation mattered. Not because Jonathan finally understood everything. Not because the conflict disappeared. But because Lisa experienced herself making contact without leaving herself.

That is practice.

## Designing the Alliance

I learned a phrase at the Coaches Training Institute that's stayed with me: *design the alliance.*

The idea is that once you've said the true thing, the next move is to make a small agreement about how the relationship will handle this going forward. The truth isn't the end. The truth is the beginning of a different arrangement.

Lisa did this without naming it. After she said *I can't keep doing that anymore,* the next move wasn't to walk away or to demand a different Jonathan. It was the explicit ask: *what I need going forward is for feedback to come with more context and more awareness of timing.* That sentence designed the new alliance. It named the truth, and it named the agreement that would honor the truth.

Most of us were never taught this second move. We were taught either to swallow the truth (and stay in the old arrangement) or to deliver the truth and walk away (no new arrangement, just the breakage). The third option is to say the truth and then negotiate the new terms. That's where the relationship actually gets remade.

Sometimes the other person can't or won't enter the new alliance. That's information too. But you don't know that until you've made the ask. Most of the time, when the truth has been clean, the other person can hear what's actually being requested, and the relationship has somewhere to go that it didn't before.

## My Own Practice

This isn't a teaching I learned from clients. It's one I had to learn, and recently.

I wrote a book about the work I do. A manifesto, really, against the unspoken contract that says I have to push myself past my limits to earn my right to belong. Then yesterday, when I finished what I thought was the final draft, within hours, a friend in marketing and I were on Zoom talking about how to bring it into the world.

We were talking about a podcast that would let people see what I write about, how I work in real time. It would show me working directly with people one-on-one. And the whole project stopped being the book and became logistics. Who would I bring on? How many sessions would it take? Would it need to be in person or on Zoom? What about cameras, lights, and microphones? Who edits? How much does it cost?

I kept agreeing to it. I kept saying yes. But I could feel my whole body bracing. It wasn't that the work scared me. It was the production. Putting it out there has a way of turning it into a job I don't really want, at a pace my system doesn't tolerate.

Underneath all the questions was that familiar drop in my stomach. And there it was, right on time: the pressure to perform.

I thought writing this book meant I was done with this, like I'd finally learned the lesson. Then, the second I finished it, I had to sell it. I was right back inside the thing I'd just spent two hundred pages naming. My mind spun out into the old familiar trap.

Option A. The doer's option. I say yes. Yes to the podcast and the plan. I turn it into a full production: gear, setup, sessions, editing, the whole thing. And to keep it moving, I do what I've always done. I override my body and treat its signals like they're in the way.

Option B. The schnorrer's option. I quietly put the book on Amazon, mention it to a few people, and act like it doesn't matter. Act like I don't need anything. Play it cool.

Either I override myself to prove I'm a doer, or I stay small and shame myself as a schnorrer.

That night I woke up at three in the morning, sleepless, hot, parched. My body was buzzing. My gut was in knots. I sat there with one question: *is this a warning about the way I know I can get when I decide something has to work?*

Part of me wanted to stay still and listen. If I didn't rush to fix it, maybe it would tell me what it was trying to protect. But another part panicked at the implication. If this was a warning, then it meant I'd already started doing the thing I always do. Gearing up to override myself to make the plan happen.

I went into the next morning's session with Alaya, my acupuncturist, tired and wired. *It feels like I'm in a no-win situation,* I told her. *Either I push and override myself, or I retreat and let it die quietly. And underneath it is the question on repeat. Why can't I just handle this?*

*I hear the question,* she said softly. *But before we answer it, let's pause. Can you describe the geography of the feeling?*

I closed my eyes. *It starts in the solar plexus, but it climbs up into my floating ribs like a tunnel.*

When I turned back toward the solar plexus and stayed with it long enough, the image shifted. The tunnel wasn't a tunnel anymore. It was armor. A shield across my chest, bronze, golden, reaching down into my quads, into the tops of my feet.

For years I'd treated that tension as a glitch. But lying there with that golden shield on, I saw what it was. Armor. The same armor I built to survive the den my father used to lock himself into. A reflex I've practiced my whole life to push panic back down. Clear the throat. Swallow hard. Puff the chest. Smile. Keep going.

The doer and the shield weren't there to make me successful. They were a tag team. One part of me pushing forward. The other part bracing over the wound so I could keep going.

Lying there, I felt the third option appear. Not prove myself, not pull back. Something subtler. Build it in a way my body could actually agree to. I didn't have to turn my office into a studio overnight. I didn't have to spend a small fortune to make the work look legitimate. I could start smaller. One conversation. One recording. A simple setup. No set. No spectacle. Just enough structure to capture what happens when I'm actually with someone.

The difference wasn't whether there were cameras. The difference was whether I had to put the shield on to be on camera. And if my body said no, then no would count. That was the third option.

I opened my laptop and started writing to my friend in marketing. At first, even the email wanted to perform. I could feel myself trying to make it sound impressive and strategic. Like this was a smart phased rollout, a lean production test, a way to validate the concept before investing more resources. All of that might have been true, but underneath it was still the old move. Dress the truth up so no one can see how vulnerable it is.

So I deleted most of it.

I sat there with my fingers on the keyboard and waited until I could feel my body again. The shield was still there, but it was thinner. I could feel the wound underneath it, which meant I could also feel the part of me that didn't want to be dragged into another heroic push.

I wrote:

*I'm still in on the podcast. I really am. But I want to start smaller than what we talked about yesterday. Could we begin with audio only and see how that feels before I go all in on cameras and production?*

There was a part of me that wanted to add three more paragraphs explaining why. I wanted to prove I wasn't backing out. I wanted to reassure him I was still serious, still ambitious, still willing to work. I wanted to make sure he didn't read it as weakness or avoidance.

That was the shield trying to sneak back in through the side door.

So I added only one more sentence.

*I want to build this in a way my body can actually say yes to.*

Then I hit send before I could turn it into a pitch deck.

For a few minutes after, I felt exposed. Not relieved. Exposed. Like I had just let someone see the part of me that couldn't muscle through.

His reply came in.

*Totally. That makes sense. Audio first is a great way to start. Let's make it easy and see what's there.*

I stared at the screen for a second.

That was it? No disappointment. No pressure. No subtle withdrawal. No *I thought you were ready to go big.* Just, yes, that makes sense. Let's make it easy.

My body didn't know what to do with that. It had braced for a fight, or at least for the need to defend my seriousness. Instead, there was nothing to defend.

The next morning, I woke up and noticed the difference immediately. I wasn't floating with relief. I wasn't suddenly free of pressure. But I also wasn't in the same three a.m. panic. My gut was quieter. My chest wasn't puffed out and armored in the same way. There was still fear, but it had room around it.

More than anything, there was dignity. Not the dignity of having conquered the whole pattern. Not the dignity of being above wanting the book to matter. I still wanted that. I wanted readers. I wanted the work to reach people. I wanted it to count. But for once, wanting it didn't require me to betray myself.

I'm clear about the next step, but I know I'm not fixed. The armor is still there. Now I know what it is.

That's what practice looks like in real life. Not a complete remaking. One honest move at a time.

## What the Body Learns

The first time you break an old pattern, it rarely feels graceful. It often feels awkward, exposed, incomplete, slightly terrifying. You may still shake. You may still want to apologize for having a need. You may still spend the next day wondering if you said too much or not enough.

Something in the body learns anyway.

*I can survive telling the truth.*

*I can let someone be disappointed and not rush to fix it.*

*I can name the impact without proving my innocence.*

*I can stay open without becoming a container for what is not mine.*

*I can remain connected to myself while in contact with you.*

That's the real change. You don't become invulnerable. You don't stop caring what the other person thinks. You don't transcend the old longing to be valued, chosen, and seen. You simply stop paying for that longing with your own disappearance.

## Repetition

Nobody gets this in one clean shot. Most of us have practiced our defenses for decades. So you'll miss it. You'll overexplain or go quiet or say yes when you mean no. Then you'll notice, recover, and try again.

That's the discipline. One small honest move repeated until it becomes a new default.

There's no version of this that doesn't include backsliding. There's no version where you finally master it and never have to practice again. The contract you signed early is going to keep showing up, in new forms, in new relationships, for the rest of your life. The work isn't to never face it again. The work is to build the muscle that recognizes it sooner and responds to it differently.

The first cycle takes the longest. The second is shorter. The fifth is faster still. Eventually, sometimes, you catch yourself mid-trade and pull back before the move is complete. That's the actual goal. Not silencing the protector. Not becoming someone who never collapses. Just shortening the loop.

## Where This Comes From

A brief lineage worth carrying.

**Werner Erhard** gave us the language of the withhold, and the practical observation that the energy spent managing the unsaid is enormous. Telling the truth gets the energy back.

**The Coaches Training Institute (CTI)** gave us the concept of designing the alliance. The truth is the beginning of a different relationship, not the end of the old one. Once said, the next move is to make the new arrangement explicit.

The somatic basis of why this all works comes from the trauma traditions you've met across this training. Peter Levine, Bessel van der Kolk, the lineages we've named. The body holds the contract. The body releases when the contract is renegotiated. The mind is informed of the release a few seconds later.

You don't need to remember the names. I'm telling you so you know the practice has weight behind it.
