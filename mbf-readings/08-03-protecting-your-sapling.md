# Protecting Your Sapling

> My advice to you is not to undertake the spiritual path. It is too difficult, too long, and too demanding. So it is best not to begin. However, if you do begin, it is best to finish.
>
> Chögyam Trungpa Rinpoche

You've just traced your own journey, the whole arc of it. Which raises the question I get asked at this point every time, which is how any of it survives an ordinary Tuesday once I'm no longer on the phone every fortnight.

And most of it doesn't. That's the plain thing to say here, and it's what this chapter is for.

Two moves. Name what you're ready to put down, then build what goes in its place.

## What you came in with

I'd start with what you've been carrying. Beliefs that once helped you survive and don't now. That you earn love by achieving, that you must never disappoint anyone, that you stay strong or stay invisible. Familiar so long they feel like facts.

The performance that kept you safe is what's keeping you small. The hiding that kept you alive is keeping you alone. The vigilance that protected you is exhausting you.

I'd honor them before you put them down, because they got you here and they did a job. But there's a point where gratitude replaces attachment and you stop needing yesterday's solution for today's problem.

Not rejection. Not judgment. *Thank you. You helped me survive. And I'm ready for something else.*

Sit with that before you read on. The part carrying the contract isn't the enemy, and integration starts by thanking it rather than banishing it.

## What integration actually is

Integration is the part nobody writes myths about. No dragon, no fig tree, no thunderclap. It's where you take what opened in you and make it the shape of your life, day after day, with nobody checking.

In my experience most of what opens here doesn't survive it. Not because it wasn't real, but because the conditions that produced it went away and nothing was put in place to protect it.

There's an instruction from Ramakrishna I come back to constantly:

> When we plant a sapling, we put a fence around it so that cattle will not eat it, and so that nobody crushes it underfoot. But when the plant grows into a large tree, the fence should be removed. Moreover, after the sapling turns into a big tree, neither can cattle eat it up nor can people crush it underfoot. The tree will drop fruit that will feed the cattle and the people who once threatened its very existence.

That's where you are. What you've grown is still a sapling, and the animals are real. Family and colleagues who liked you better as you were. Old habits that went quiet rather than away. The pull of comfort on a hard morning.

So I want a fence, held up by three posts. Take one away and it falls.

## Post 1: Patience and Persistence

You have only just begun, and I mean that.

You can't expect yourself to be free of reactivity. You will get triggered. You will fall back into old patterns. You will lose the felt sense and react before you remember to pause. This is not failure. This is what the early years of a real practice actually look like.

It took a lifetime to build the patterns that send you into reactivity, and it takes longer than eight modules to undo them. I've seen people work with this for years before it started to feel like second nature. That's the real timeframe, and anybody telling you otherwise is selling something.

I pair the two because each one fails without the other. Patience without persistence is just waiting, and hoping the work does itself. Persistence without patience is brittle. You push, expect progress on your schedule, get frustrated when it doesn't come, and burn out or collapse into self-criticism. I've watched a lot of people start strong and quit inside a year for exactly that, having mistaken impatience for commitment.

Together they're the long view, and the first time I watch somebody really get it, something relaxes in them. The pressure to have arrived already comes off. You've got the rest of your life for this, and I don't think that's bad news. It's the most generous timeline anybody has offered you.

I used to imagine enlightenment as a kind of Oscar ceremony. Called to a stage, beautiful people handing me an award, thanking everyone who helped, and then suffused in light and living forever in bliss. I gave that up when I noticed the Buddha kept getting old and sick and died of food poisoning. The point isn't a destination where suffering ends. It's the capacity to meet suffering, including your own, with more grace than you used to.

That's a lifelong project, and it deserves lifelong patience.

## Post 2: Trust Your Own Knowing

The second is harder to describe and I think it matters more.

There's a word in Buddhist practice, *ehipassiko*, meaning roughly come and see for yourself. Nothing here is meant to be taken on faith. The Buddha reportedly told his students not to believe him because he'd said it. Try it. See what happens. Keep what works.

That's harder than it sounds, because we're trained almost from birth to look outside ourselves for confirmation we're doing it right. Grades, reviews, likes, the approval of parents and partners and bosses. We scan faces for whether we're okay. I'm asking you to put that machinery down and use a different instrument, which is the felt sense of whether your life is getting closer to what you love.

It's already in you, and most of what I've been doing across these modules is helping you hear it again under the noise.

So here's the question I'd keep asking once this ends, rather than looking for somebody to answer it. *Are the choices I'm making honoring what I love and the gifts I'm here to give?*

And some of what I've given you won't fit. Set it on a shelf rather than throwing it out, because some of it becomes useful years later when your life finally makes it legible.

## Post 3: Your Daily Practice

The third post is the simplest and the one most easily abandoned.

Sit down. Set a timer. Close your eyes. Follow your breath. Fifteen minutes minimum, every day, and I'd keep the time and the place more or less the same.

That's the whole instruction and I don't have an advanced version. The monks who've been at it for decades are doing the same simple thing, on the eight thousandth day rather than the eighth.

What I'm after with it is the capacity to notice small things. The shallowness of your breath when something's bothering you that you haven't named. The tightening in your shoulders five minutes before you snap at somebody. None of that comes from books. It comes from sitting with your own body long enough to recognize its weather.

And your critic will give you excellent reasons to skip it. *I'll do it later. I had a hard day. I'm too anxious to meditate right now.* That voice isn't your friend. It's the part that wants things as they were, and every time you sit down anyway, in spite of it, you weaken its hold a little further.

So I'd fix the time, fix the place, and show up, and I mean especially on the days when the sitting itself feels completely useless, because the showing up is the practice and the meditation is very nearly a side effect of it.

Two notes. Retreats will move you further in a few days than months of fifteen-minute sits at home will. And find a *sangha* if you can, because sitting with other people holds you to it in a way that sitting on your own never quite does.

## What the fence is for

If the three posts hold, the sapling grows.

You'll need it a while, and then the proportions shift. The reactivity gets less intense. The new responses feel less like effort. Practice stops being a discipline and becomes a refuge. And people start responding to you differently, because you're different.

I'd add that the fence isn't permanent, and Ramakrishna is clear about that. At some point it comes down or it becomes a constraint instead of a protection. That's a question for years from now.

And the last line of the parable is what I'd leave you with. The tree feeds the cattle that would have eaten it.

What you're protecting now, you'll hand back eventually. For now I just want the fence.

## Summary

- Integration is where most of what opens in this work gets quietly lost. A fence around the new growth is what prevents it.
- Three posts: patience and persistence, trust in your own knowing, daily practice.
- The fence isn't permanent, but for now it's what keeps the sapling alive.

## Action

Identify your fence: the time and place of your daily sit, and one practice you'll commit to in the first thirty days after this program ends.
