# Protecting Your Sapling

> My advice to you is not to undertake the spiritual path. It is too difficult, too long, and too demanding. This is not a picnic. It will ask everything of you. So it is best not to begin. However, if you do begin, it is best to finish.
>
> Chögyam Trungpa Rinpoche

This chapter does two things. First, it asks you to name what you're now ready to put down. Then it turns to what gets built in its place.

You looked back in the last reading. Now we look at what to do with what you've grown.

But before we build anything new, name what you're now ready to put down.

## What you came in with

Many of us spend years carrying beliefs that once helped us survive but no longer help us thrive. Some carry the belief that they must earn love through achievement. Others believe they must never disappoint anyone. Some believe they must always remain strong. Others believe they must stay invisible to stay safe.

Over time, these beliefs become so familiar that they feel like facts.

Meaningful growth often reveals that these strategies are no longer necessary. The performance that once kept you safe is now the thing keeping you small. The hiding that once kept you alive is now the thing keeping you alone. The vigilance that once protected you is now exhausting you.

There is wisdom in honoring the old adaptations. They got you here. They served a purpose. But there comes a time when gratitude must replace attachment. A time when we recognize that we no longer need yesterday's solutions for today's challenges.

This is one of the deepest forms of completion. Not rejection. Not judgment. Simply acknowledgment.

*Thank you. You helped me survive. And now I am ready for something new.*

Sit with that for a moment, before you read on. The version of you that's been carrying the old contract is not the enemy. It got you here. The work of integration begins with thanking it, not banishing it.

Then we build what comes next.

## What integration actually is

The work of integration is the part of the journey nobody writes myths about. It's not dramatic. There's no dragon, no fig tree, no thunderclap of awakening. It's the part where you take what opened up in you and make it the actual shape of your life, day after day, in the absence of a coach checking in on you.

Most spiritual breakthroughs don't survive the integration phase. Not because the breakthroughs weren't real, but because the conditions that produced them disappeared, and nothing was put in place to protect what they produced.

There's an instruction from the nineteenth-century Indian mystic Ramakrishna that gets at this exactly:

> When we plant a sapling, we put a fence around it so that cattle will not eat it, and so that nobody crushes it underfoot. But when the plant grows into a large tree, the fence should be removed and taken away. If the fence is not removed in time, it might even hinder the growth of the tree. The trunk may even get trapped within the fence. Moreover, after the sapling turns into a big tree, neither can cattle eat it up fully nor can people crush it under their feet accidentally. The tree will drop fruit that will feed the cattle and the people who once threatened its very existence.

This is exactly your situation right now. The insights, the calm, the clearer sense of yourself that you've cultivated over these months: all of that is still a sapling. The animals that want to graze it down are real, and they come in many forms. Family members and colleagues who liked you better the way you used to be. Old habits that haven't gone anywhere, just gone quiet. The pull of comfort on a hard morning, when meditation feels like one more thing on the list.

You need a fence.

The fence is held up by three posts. If any one of them is missing, the fence falls.

## Post 1: Patience and Persistence

You have just begun.

You can't expect yourself to be free of reactivity. You will get triggered. You will fall back into old patterns. You will lose the felt sense and react before you remember to pause. This is not failure. This is what the early years of a real practice actually look like.

It took you a lifetime to build the patterns that send you into reactivity. It will take more than eight modules to undo them. You may work with this material for many years before you start to feel like it has become second nature. That's the timeframe. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a lie.

Patience and persistence are paired because each one fails without the other.

Patience without persistence is passive. You're just waiting. You're hoping the work will somehow do itself while you tolerate the wait. It won't. The fence won't build itself.

Persistence without patience is brittle. You push and push, expect rapid progress, get frustrated when the progress doesn't come on your schedule, and eventually burn out or collapse into self-criticism. I've watched many people start strong on the path and quit within a year for exactly this reason. They mistook impatience for commitment.

Together, patience and persistence are the long view. You're going to keep doing this work, and you're going to be gentle with yourself about how slowly the changes accumulate. Both at once. The first time you really get this in your bones, something relaxes. The pressure to have arrived already, to have figured it out, to be done, lifts off you. You realize you have the rest of your life to do this, and that's not bad news. It's the most generous timeline anyone has ever offered you.

I used to imagine enlightenment as a kind of Oscar ceremony. I'd be called to a stage, beautiful people would hand me an award, I'd thank everyone who helped me along the way, and then I'd be suffused in light and live forever in bliss. I gave that fantasy up when I noticed that even the Buddha kept getting old, kept getting sick, and eventually died of food poisoning. The point of the path is not to reach a destination where suffering ends. The point is to develop the capacity to meet suffering, including your own, with more grace than you used to.

That's a lifelong project. It deserves lifelong patience.

## Post 2: Trust Your Own Knowing

The second post is harder to describe but, in some ways, more important.

In Buddhist practice, there is a word, *ehipassiko*, which translates roughly as *come and see for yourself*. It's the principle that nothing in this work should be taken on faith. The Buddha himself reportedly told his students not to believe his teachings because he said them, or because they were tradition, or because they sounded good. Try them. See what happens. Keep what works for your actual life. Set the rest aside.

This is the most radical thing about the path you've been walking. It is not asking you to believe. It is asking you to experiment and to trust the results of your own experiments.

That's harder than it sounds. We're trained, almost from birth, to look outside ourselves for confirmation that we're doing things right. Grades, performance reviews, likes, the approval of parents and partners and bosses. We learn to scan other people's faces for whether we're okay. The path asks you to put that machinery down and develop a different kind of instrument: the felt sense of whether your life is becoming more aligned with what you actually love.

That instrument is already in you. It's been in you the whole time. The work of these modules has been, in part, to help you hear it again under all the noise.

So here is the question I want you to keep asking, after this program ends, instead of looking for someone to tell you the answer:

*Are the choices I'm making honoring what I love, what I care about, and the gifts I'm here to give? Am I more connected to my higher purpose this month than I was last month?*

Some of what we've covered will resonate deeply with you. Some of it won't. That's appropriate. *Ehipassiko* gives you permission to set aside what doesn't fit your life right now. Just don't throw it out. Set it on a shelf. Some teachings have a way of becoming useful years after you first encountered them, when something in your life finally makes them legible. Keep what works. Let the rest wait.

## Post 3: Your Daily Practice

The third post is the simplest and the one most easily abandoned.

Sit down. Set a timer. Close your eyes. Follow your breath. Fifteen minutes minimum, every day, more or less the same time, more or less the same place.

That's the whole instruction. There's nothing to figure out. There's no advanced version. Even the monks who have been doing this for decades are doing this same simple thing. They're just doing it on the eight thousandth day instead of the eighth.

What this builds, over time, is the capacity to notice. Subtle things. The shallowness of your breath when something is bothering you that you haven't named yet. The tightening in your shoulders five minutes before you snap at someone. The heat in your cheeks when you're embarrassed and pretending you're not. None of this awareness comes from books. It comes from sitting with your own body, every day, for long enough that you start to recognize its weather.

Your inner critic will offer you many compelling reasons to skip the practice. *I'll do it later. I had a hard day. I'll start fresh tomorrow. I'm too anxious to meditate right now.* That voice is not your friend. It is the part of you that wants to keep things the way they used to be. Every time you sit down anyway, you weaken its grip. Every time you let it talk you out of practice, you strengthen it.

Find a fixed time. Find a fixed place. Show up. Even on the days when the meditation itself feels useless, especially on those days, the showing up is the practice. The meditation is almost a side effect.

Two practical notes. First, retreats are the single most powerful way to deepen a practice. A few days of silent sitting will move you forward more than a few months of fifteen-minute sessions at home. Find a community of practice if you can. The Buddhist tradition calls this *sangha*. Sitting with other people, even just once a week, holds you to your practice in a way solo sitting cannot. We'll talk more about this in the next reading.

## What the fence is for

If the three posts hold, the sapling grows.

For a while, you'll still need the fence. You'll still notice the pull of old patterns, still need patience with yourself when you fall short, still need *ehipassiko* to remind you to trust your own felt sense, still need the daily sit to keep the awareness alive.

Then, slowly, the proportions begin to shift. The reactivity gets less intense. The new responses start to feel less like effort and more like who you are. The patience becomes its own quiet pleasure. Practice stops being a discipline and becomes a kind of refuge. You begin to notice that the people around you are responding to you differently because you are different.

## And eventually, the tree.

The fence isn't permanent. Ramakrishna's instruction is clear about this. At some point, the fence has to come down, or it becomes a constraint instead of a protection. But that's a question for many years from now. For the next phase of your life, the fence is what keeps the new growth alive. Build it well.

The last thing the parable says is the part I want to leave you with: *The tree will drop fruit that will feed the cattle and the people who once threatened its very existence.*

What you're protecting now, you'll eventually offer back. The patience you're learning will become patience for someone else. The awareness you're cultivating will become a steadier presence in the lives of the people you love. The practice you're building will, in some quiet way, leave the world a little less reactive than you found it.

That's the long arc. For now, just build the fence.

## Summary

- The integration phase is where most spiritual breakthroughs are quietly lost. A fence around the new growth is what prevents that.
- The fence has three posts: patience and persistence, trust in your own knowing, and daily practice.
- The fence is not permanent. But for now, it's what keeps the sapling alive.

## Action

Identify your fence: the time and place of your daily sit, and one practice you'll commit to in the first thirty days after this program ends.
