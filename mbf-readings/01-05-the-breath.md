# The Breath

*This is **S** in **SENSE**: slow the breath. The first move.*

Last chapter I showed you the machinery: the contract, the time travel, the ache underneath the performance. Which raises the obvious question, and it's probably the one you're holding right now. If all of that is running in me, what do I actually do about it?

This is the answer, and it's smaller than you'd expect. Before you can meet any of it with curiosity, before beginner's mind, before you can even hear what your body has been telling you, the nervous system has to settle. You can't do this work in fight-or-flight, and you can't go near what's tender from inside the panic that's trying to outrun it.

So we start with the breath. It's the one lever your nervous system hands you that works in real time, taking you out of the speed of survival and into the room where presence is possible.

And everything else here rests on it. Every practice, every moment of staying with what comes up, every honest conversation. All of it starts here.

## The Straw Breath

The practice I start everyone on is the straw breath. It's simple and mechanical and it works.

Inhale slowly through the nose for a count of five. Then purse your lips, as though you were blowing through a straw, and exhale slowly through the mouth for a count of seven.

The longer exhale is the key, and I want to be clear it isn't about taking deep breaths or filling your lungs. It's the slow, smooth, continuous flow. Letting the air move evenly, without strain, without holding.

And I'm not asking you to relax. You're sending your nervous system a physical signal that it's safe to slow down.

## What's Actually Happening

You've probably been told to just breathe enough times to find it dismissive, and I don't blame you. So let me be specific about why this one works.

When you breathe slowly and smoothly with a longer exhale, you engage the diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle just beneath your lungs. Slow, even breathing puts the diaphragm into a continuous, rhythmic motion. As the diaphragm moves this way, it stimulates a network of stretch receptors in your lungs and pressure receptors in your chest cavity.

These receptors send signals up the vagus nerve to the brainstem, and the message is this: we aren't under attack, the system can downshift.

In response, your body begins to move out of sympathetic activation, the fight-or-flight state of high alert, and into parasympathetic rest, the state in which digestion, repair, reflection, and curiosity become possible.

It isn't instant. But over five to ten minutes of slow, continuous breathing the biology changes. Heart rate slows. Blood pressure eases. Your brain updates its read on the moment, and a mind that had been running on the assumption of threat begins to find the threat isn't there.

That's the moment the work becomes possible. I'll take you through it on our first call so you can feel what I mean rather than take my word for it.

I spent years training in Chinese medicine partly to understand this, and what I
find striking is how little the mechanism cares whose language you use for it.
The old texts described it one way and the physiology describes it another, and
they're pointing at the same lever. I'd rather you feel it than take either
account on faith.

If you like knowing the technical name, this pace, roughly five breaths a minute, is what researchers call resonance or coherent breathing. It's the rate at which heart and breath synchronize on their own. You don't need the term to benefit from the practice.

If you want to go further on the science, James Nestor's *Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art* is the best popular treatment I know of.

## Why This Matters for Everything That Follows

Most of my clients arrive with their nervous systems in low-grade activation, and I'd say the world they work in rewards it. Speed feels productive. Vigilance feels responsible. What it costs is access to the parts of you that only turn up when you've settled: the curiosity, the presence, the ability to feel what's actually here.

I can't get you to beginner's mind from inside fight-or-flight. A mind bracing for the next thing can't also be open to what's in front of it, and a body scanning for danger can't feel its own quiet ache.

So this isn't a relaxation technique. It's the precondition for everything after it.

I'll tell you my own stake in this. My gut was a wreck for years, and I tried herbs and supplements and acupuncture and pharmaceuticals before any of it helped. The breath is the piece I still use every day, and it's the one I have most dialed in.

## Lessons from the Forest

None of this is new. I've been practising in this lineage since 1993, and people worked it out a very long time before either of us turned up.

In ancient India, students walked into the forest for days to find a teacher, and the arrangement was simple. They stayed, they listened, they watched. And the teaching came back again and again to the breath. That's the world of the Upanishads, oral teachings passed from teacher to student across centuries. They weren't a manual of techniques. They were a record of what people had come to know about mind and body and the force that animated both. They called it *prana*, and the breath, for them, wasn't only what kept you alive. It was where mind and body met. Watch one and you start to see the other.

Later it became technique. Around the 15th century a teacher named Svatmarama compiled the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, and by then the tradition had a precise vocabulary for what happens between breath and nervous system. *Prana*, the life force carried on the inhale. *Apana*, the grounding stillness on the exhale. Too much prana and you're restless. Too much apana and you're sluggish. The breath working evenly between them is what brings the system into balance.

That line arrives in the twentieth century in Tirumalai Krishnamacharya, sometimes called the father of modern yoga. I write in the book about the grainy 1938 film of him, in a cotton loincloth, moving like someone for whom breath and body were one instrument. Nearly every school of yoga you've heard of came from his students.

What he taught is that the breath is a mirror for the mind. Scattered breath, agitated mind. Lengthen and refine the flow and it becomes your main tool for steadying attention. And everything you'll do with me, from the straw breath today to the harder stayings-with later on, sits on that one principle.

So the old instruction, *open your mind and let go of what you think you know*, only works once the system has settled enough to look. Beginner's mind, which is next, is first a kind of seeing. You look at where your mind is already closed, already certain, where your beliefs narrowed without your noticing. And you can't see any of that from inside the noise.

## Summary

- The breath is the doorway from sympathetic activation into parasympathetic rest.
- The straw breath uses a slow inhale through the nose and a longer exhale through pursed lips. It engages the diaphragm, which signals safety to the brainstem via the vagus nerve.
- Over five to ten minutes of practice, the biology actually shifts. The system settles. Curiosity becomes possible.
- Everything that follows in this work depends on this settling: meeting yourself with beginner's mind, listening to your body's signals, finding the third option in difficult moments.
- The breath is what makes all of it available.

## What It's Actually Like

I should tell you what to expect, because I've sat with this every morning for
thirty years and I still have days where it does nothing.

Some sits are quiet and something settles and you'll feel it. Some sits you'll
spend the whole ten minutes making a list in your head, and you'll come out
thinking you did it wrong. You didn't. I've had weeks of the second kind.

What I've learned is that the days I least want to sit are usually the days it
matters most, and that I'm the worst judge of a sit while I'm in it. So don't
grade them. Just keep showing up, and tell me what you notice.

## Action

Begin a daily breath practice. The details, including the app I'd like you to use, are in **01.05A Breath Practice**. Plan to sit for ten to fifteen minutes a day.

And through your day, return to the breath whenever you think of it. Three deliberate breaths before a meeting, before a hard email, on the way into a conversation you've been avoiding. I want each return building your nervous system's capacity to find its own balance.

Notice what's different in the first few minutes after a breath practice. That state, settled, curious, present, is what we'll work from in the next lesson on Beginner's Mind.
