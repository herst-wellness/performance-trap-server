# Awareness

*This is **E·N** in **SENSE**: enter the body, name what arises. The second and third moves.*

In the Welcome I said awareness is what lets you set the lens down, and then I left it as a word. So here's what it looks like working, and I'll use myself because it's the clearest example I have.

A few weeks ago I was being interviewed for a podcast, and the host asked how I was navigating right now, with the book about to launch and more eyes on me than I'd ever had. As I started to answer I noticed I was anxious. Not the show-up kind. The deeper one that's run underneath my work for as long as I've been doing it.

And twenty years ago I'd have done what I always did. Pushed through, made the anxious part invisible so the smart part could be seen.

What I said instead, into the microphone, in front of the audience: *"I still feel anxious. But I'm in relationship to the anxiety here. I feel something in my chest, in my belly. I'm not trying to stop it. I can feel that as part of the conversation."*

Then I kept talking.

That's the shift this chapter is about. The old move was *make it go away*. The new one is *let it be here while I keep going*, and that's a different nervous system entirely. It took me thirty years to get from one to the other.

## What Just Happened

For most of my life, when something difficult arose I was inside it. The anxiety wasn't something I was experiencing, it was something I was. *I'm anxious. I'm overwhelmed. I'm not okay.* No gap between sensation and identification, and most people live there. It isn't a flaw. It's what happens when nobody showed you there was an alternative.

Awareness is the part of you that knows there is one. It's where *me* starts becoming *a part of me*.

## The Witness

The contemplative traditions of India call it the witness, and it isn't a better personality or a perfected self or an ideal you're failing to reach. It's the capacity to know experience, the part of you that notices the thought without being the thought. Clouds move through the sky, and the sky doesn't go anywhere while they pass.

I met this idea in 1993, in Maui, and it changed the direction of my life. I was doing yoga in the mornings, riding a bicycle out to a beach or a waterfall in Hana, then lying on a futon for hours feeling what was going on in my body. My teacher, David Williams, handed me a book by an Indian mystic named Dada Gavand, called *Towards the Unknown*. Four lines on the back of it:

> Meditation is choiceless perception
> Uninvolved, all around attention
> Undiluted by thoughts and emotions
> Unlimited by technique and tradition

Choiceless. Uninvolved. That was the part I hadn't understood. I'd been treating meditation as something you get good at, another skill to acquire, and Gavand was describing something with no technique in it at all. Just watching, without choosing what you watch and without climbing into it. I brought the book to David and he said yes, that's really what yoga is. The old distinction between the one who sees and everything that's seen. The watcher isn't part of the show.

But what stayed with me wasn't the idea. It was that lying on the floor in Hana, I found I could do it. It wasn't conceptual, it was embodied, and it stayed. The first thing I ever learned to do on the inside was watch.

So that capacity is already in you. It isn't something you build. It's a natural part of your consciousness that most of us were never told about, and this module is about cultivating it.

And that gap is what I meant on the podcast by being in relationship to the anxiety.

## The Moves

You don't think your way from being inside an experience to being in relationship with it. It happens through four specific moves.

**The U-turn.** When you're caught in a story about a feeling, the mind wants to keep talking. Turn around and go to the body instead. Where is it? What is it like? Tight, hot, heavy, hollow, sharp, dull? The body is what's happening and the story is what your mind is doing on top of it.

**Stepping back an inch.** Step back about an inch from your own body and watch the sensation from just outside it. Not far. Just far enough that the feeling is something happening rather than something you are. People describe staticky energy, or a heavy stone, or a tight band. That small gap is the witness.

**Keeping it company.** Put your attention around the sensation and breathe with it. Not changing it, not analyzing it, staying close the way you'd stay close to a friend having a hard day. It's harder than it sounds and it gets a whole module of its own later on.

**The rephrase.** *I am anxious* becomes *there's anxiety here*. Read both out loud and notice what changes in your body. This one is the Buddhist practice of noting, which is much older than any of us, and Daniel Siegel's phrase for why it works is name it to tame it.

None of these are mine. The U-turn is close to what the philosopher Eugene Gendlin called the felt sense, and I mention it so you know the practice has weight behind it.

None of them need special conditions either. You can do them at a desk, in a meeting, in the middle of the night, and the journal takes you through all four with a feeling of your own, which is where you'll learn them. Two have names in SENSE: the U-turn is **Enter the Body**, and stepping back and naming what you see is **Name the Quality**. With the breath, that's three of the five letters in your hands.

## Why This Is Hard

None of this is hard intellectually. People understand the words fine. It's hard because the loudest voice in most people is the inner critic, and the inner critic doesn't sound like a voice. It sounds like you.

A client I'll call Marcus had sold his company for a number that should have set him free, and for six months it did. Then he trusted a wealth manager who pitched him a once in a lifetime startup that turned out to be a scam, and the money was gone.

And that was when the voice came back. *How could you be so naive. Your first success was just luck. You don't know what you're doing.*

He'd been hearing some version of it his whole life. He moved from India to a small American town as a kid, and on the first day the other children laughed at his accent and his clothes, then laughed again when he stumbled reading aloud. He didn't tell his parents, who were working too hard. He told himself to toughen up.

Forty years on, the voice was still there and he had never separated himself from it. When it spoke he heard himself speaking, and when it said *you're a failure*, he was one. It never introduced itself. It never said *hi, I'm the part of you that learned to put you down before anyone else could.* It just spoke, and it felt like the truth.

What changed in our work wasn't the volume. What changed was that he learned to say *I notice self-doubt* instead of *I am a failure*. The first sentence has a witness in it. The second is a verdict.

We'll be with the critic properly in the next chapter. For now, start noticing that the loudest voice in your head isn't necessarily you.

## The Space for Choice

What awareness gives you, more than anything, is the small pause between sensation and reaction. It doesn't sound like much. It changes everything.

For most of my life I had one strategy for a difficult feeling, which was to get rid of it, and Module 1 is the account of where that ended up. So what I've been learning since is the thing I used to think was the opposite of strength. To stop trying to stop the feeling.

The feelings haven't gone away. I still get the small tightening in my chest and the catch in my breath that mean something in me is bracing. What I do now is notice it's happening, breathe from where I am rather than from where I think I should be, and ask how I move from this place. *From this place* is the pause, and my whole nervous system reorganizes around it.

It isn't dramatic. It's the moment you notice something is happening instead of acting automatically, and find you have more than one way to be with it.

And underneath all of it, what's being rebuilt is your body as a source you can trust. For most of your life its signals were the problem. Anxiety meant something was wrong with you, tightness meant push through. Everything from here reverses that.

## Before You Put This Down

Look away and see how many of these you can say back. Getting them wrong is fine and still does the work.

- Most of us live inside our experience rather than in relationship to it. No gap.
- Awareness is the capacity to know experience without being it.
- Four moves: the U-turn, stepping back an inch, keeping it company, the rephrase.
- The hardest thing to catch is the inner critic, because most people hear it as themselves.
- Awareness doesn't remove feelings. It makes the pause where reaction becomes response.
