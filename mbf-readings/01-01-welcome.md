# Welcome

Welcome to Mind/Body Foundations.

People arrive here in different states and describe it in their own words, but it's usually some version of this.

You're anxious a good deal of the time and can't always say what about. There's a pressure that doesn't let up, and something in you stays braced all day.

You're stuck. Not short of options. Stuck in the sense that you can see the situation clearly, know the sensible move, and still can't make it.

You're tired. Not the kind sleep fixes, and you've been tired long enough that you've stopped calling it anything. And when you do stop you don't rest, you go flat.

There's a running list in your head of people who might be let down, and you manage it all day. Whatever you did, it wasn't quite enough, and the next thing is already waiting.

And from the outside none of it shows. You deliver. You produce. You make it look easy.

Some of it takes longer to say out loud. The guilt about any hour that wasn't productive. The resentment. The loneliness of being the one everybody leans on. Most people don't lead with those and you don't have to either.

I call this the performance trap.

It's the pressure to prove you're enough. It's the unspoken contract you signed long before you had words for it: that worth is earned, that rest is earned, that parts of you have to be left behind to stay valued and relied on.

It's older than your career. It isn't a productivity problem and it isn't a discipline problem. It's a survival strategy you built when you learned, very young, that being yourself wasn't enough.

This work isn't psychotherapy. There's overlap, and if you're seeing a therapist, keep seeing them. I'd rather you had more support than less. What I'm doing is teaching you skills you'll be able to use on your own.

## What It Sounds Like

Underneath all of that there are sentences, quiet enough that you may never have said one out loud. These are the ones I hear most.

*I can't let go of any of it.*

*It's too much.*

*I'm angrier than I want to be.*

*There's nothing in it anymore.*

*I don't want to be a burden.*

*I can't say no to this.*

*They're going to find out I don't know what I'm doing.*

*I'm fine.*

Most people recognize more than one. None of them is a character flaw. Each was learned in a moment when you found out connection had conditions.

## How the Trap Got Built

We arrive wired for connection. Not as a preference, as a requirement, because a baby can't feed or protect itself. The nervous system learns one thing early. Closeness means safety. Distance means danger.

There's an experiment called the Still Face. A parent and infant sit face to face, smiling and making sounds, and then the parent goes neutral and holds a blank face. The baby reaches harder, then fusses, turns away, and starts to soothe itself. When the response doesn't come reliably, the system stops expecting it and learns to handle things alone.

But there's a second piece, and it's the one that keeps you in the room. A double bind is two contradictory messages at once. The words say *I love you* while the body stiffens as you come close. A child can't name that contradiction without threatening the bond.

So they do the safest thing available. They stop trusting their own reading of the room and become easier, quieter, more useful, whatever keeps the connection.

And over time it goes automatic. The strategy stops feeling like a strategy and starts feeling like your personality.

## The Ache

Every time you override what you actually feel, the gut no, the chest tightness, the request for help you swallow, it leaves an imprint. Not as a memory in your mind but in your tissue. The tight jaw, the braced shoulders, the *I'm fine* when nothing is fine.

Year after year it adds up. It shows up as the ache you can't quite name, the heaviness behind the wins, the flatness after a goal, the loneliness inside the success. And there's almost always a second voice with it, the inner critic, the part that keeps the performance running by attacking any sign you're slowing down. So you end up quietly self-attacking as well as quietly hurting.

That's what we'll be working with. Not the symptoms, the pattern underneath, and not by analyzing it but by learning to stop overriding it.

## A Story

A client of mine came in still upset about a small administrative slip on a trip, an unforced error that set him off in a way he recognized from his whole life. Tracing it back, he landed on a memory from when he was seven, lying awake thinking that if he'd noticed that morning the ketchup was running low, he could have prevented his father's blowup at his mother that night.

That vigilance became the engine of his career. It made him excellent under pressure, and it made joy almost impossible to reach, because every moment of pleasure felt like borrowed time.

The opening came when he could tell the difference between his sense of duty, which he didn't want to lose, and the hypervigilance that rode along with it, which was killing him. They'd felt like one thing his entire life, and seeing them as two was the first move toward letting one stay and letting the other rest.

## Background

I didn't come to this work because I had life figured out. I came to it because mine came apart early.

When I was seventeen, in my first week of college, my brother took his life by jumping off the Golden Gate Bridge. I'd grown up in a house where sensitivity got shut down and performance got rewarded, and I could walk into any room and sense which version of me was most acceptable, most impressive, least threatening. That became my identity, and after he took his life I didn't drop it. I still knew how to deliver. Inside it felt like watching my life through glass.

A few months later everything went into freefall and I entered the Hoffman Institute, a week-long residential program about the conditioning we pick up in childhood. Every morning somebody asked me how I felt, which I had no ready answer for. And that's where I first made contact with the younger, frightened part of me the grief had exposed.

Then a friend invited me to Hawaii to study with a yoga teacher. I was standing in paradise and couldn't feel any of it. The first day he took me through ten sun salutations, and I was stiff and breathless the whole way. Then he had me lie down and said something I've never forgotten. You have a pharmacy inside of you, and if you let yourself feel what's happening in your body, all of the healing properties will go to the different places inside. For the first three or four minutes nothing happened. Then I felt a vice-like quality in my chest, and I felt it crack open, and tears came down my cheeks. I'd been talking about that grief for a year. I had never once felt it. I stayed six months instead of two weeks.

That sent me to India, and I've been working with my own body ever since. My practice started in 1993. I began working on other people's bodies in 1997 as a Thai massage therapist and yoga teacher, trained in Chinese medicine in 2003 and practiced acupuncture for seventeen years, and started coaching in 2008.

And the same people kept turning up. High achievers, perfectionists, people who looked good on paper and were privately falling apart. So I built a method for them, and this is it. It comes out of what I lived, and out of years with people trained to succeed at everything except being with themselves.

## What's in Store

We work in two directions.

Inward, we rebuild your contact with the signals your body has been sending, the ones you got too good at overriding. You learn to slow down, name what's there, and stay with it long enough for something to move.

Outward, we take that contact into your relationships. You learn to catch the moment the old pattern fires, right before you swallow something to keep the peace, and find a third option that doesn't ask you to abandon yourself.

It's slow, and it builds capacity the way training builds muscle, through small repeated reps. What you're hoping for won't arrive all at once.

## Next Steps

Two things will help most.

The first is coming in as a student. You're used to being good at things, and this isn't about being capable. Being open doesn't mean ignoring what you know. It means making room for what you don't.

The second is the daily sit. If you've decided you're bad at meditating, let that go, because this isn't about being good at it. Fifteen minutes to start, building toward thirty. What else I'm asking, and what you can ask of me, is in Session Preparation.

This won't always feel good and you won't always want to do it. Some weeks it'll feel like nothing is happening, and some weeks the ache gets louder before it quiets. That isn't a sign it's going wrong, and it's the part nobody warned me about either.

And I'm not going to stuff any of this down your throat, so if something I hand you doesn't resonate, set it aside. Use me between sessions. I'm an open book, and the questions that come up when you're on your own are usually the good ones.

If you stay with it you'll start to recognize yourself again, underneath the momentum and the part that's been holding it all together. You don't have to become someone new. We're making it possible, slowly, to stop leaving yourself out.

I'm looking forward to walking this with you.
