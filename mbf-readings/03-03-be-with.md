# Be With

*This is the second **S** in **SENSE**: stay. The fourth move.*

Last chapter ended at feeling tone, the second of the five steps. That's where a hand can still get into the loop, and it's the one place most people never look.

Which leaves the only question that matters, and it's the one I get asked every time I teach this. What do you do there? The window is a fraction of a second wide, you're already flooded when it opens, and everything in you is built to be somewhere else by the time it closes.

Here's the move I'm going to ask you for. You stay with it.

Not analyze it, not breathe it away, not name it and move on. You keep the sensation company while it does whatever it's going to do, and you don't leave.

## Why It's So Hard

You know the moment I'm pointing at. Feedback lands wrong, or a text from someone you love has the wrong tone in it, and your chest grips, heat comes up your neck.

And then, in most of us, a flash of activity. Five emails answered fast. A tab opened. A drink poured. Something defensive said, or something said to smooth it over. Anything that gives back a feeling of competence fast enough that the ache never gets felt.

That's the contract firing, the one I named in *Why We Perform*. None of those moves is about the present moment. They're about an old one, where the rule got signed that parts of you have to be left behind to stay safe in the room. So you're not reacting to the email. Your body has time traveled and is running the old terms on new conditions.

Staying is how you decline to sign it again.

## Where I Got This

I want to tell you where I got this, because it isn't mine and it'll mean more to you if you know whose it is.

When I came back from India, after studying with Pattabhi Jois and doing my first long retreat in Sri Lanka, my life was rearranging itself around what I'd seen. In that stretch I found Pema Chödrön, and her audiobook *When Things Fall Apart* became a good friend. I'd put it on in the car and she'd keep naming the one move I most wanted to make and most wanted to avoid.

> Those are the moments that teach us to perk up and lean in when we feel we'd rather collapse and back away.

I've carried that sentence in my body for two decades now. When I feel the urge to collapse, I try not to collapse. When I feel the urge to back away, I try not to back away. I stay, and I let the wave come, and that's the whole of what I'm asking you for here.

I don't think it's toughness and I don't think it's endurance. It's quieter than either of those. It's choosing to keep yourself company at the moment you'd usually leave.

## The Return, Turned Inward

There's a piece of the Still Face research I left behind in Module 1 and I want it back now. Most people remember the blank face. The half I find they forget is what comes after it.

The parent's face softens. They smile again, they reach, and the baby who had gone into withdrawal comes back. What that teaches is not what the still face taught. It teaches that a break doesn't mean alone, and that we find each other again.

That's what staying is doing inside you, except that here the parent and the baby are the same person. The part of you that learned to handle it alone is still in there, still doing the job. And every time you stay with a feeling instead of overriding it, you're the one who came back.

I want you to have the weight of that one. In most of the moments when you needed someone to come back, nobody did. So now you get to be the one who does, not as a way of speaking but as something happening in your body while it happens.

## What You'll Meet First

This has a shape you'll recognize once you're in it. When you first turn toward what's firing, what you meet usually isn't the tender thing. It's the protection. The mind speeds up. You go blank, or hot, or slightly sick, or your hand is on your phone before you noticed reaching. That layer is a protector, the same crew you met on the bus, and its job is to keep you away from what's underneath. In the body it tends to show up as something hard. A clamp around the ribs. A band across the chest. Something that says, without any words, that you have to handle this.

I wrote about one of my own in *The Performance Trap*. I was sitting with a wave of panic, and the first thing I found wasn't the panic at all. It was a sensation I came to think of as a golden shield wrapped around my ribs. Polished, protective, solid. I stayed with it, and over some minutes the shield softened into something else. The hardness became tenderness. The shield became wounded flesh. I hadn't done anything to it. I'd only stopped leaving.

That shift is what I'm pointing you toward. You don't make it happen. You make room for it, and it happens on its own schedule, or it doesn't happen today and you come back tomorrow. And when you do find the ache, there's nothing to do with it. You don't have to fix it or name it. Letting it be there is the whole of it.

## Small Doses

One piece of trauma work makes this safe, and I'd rather hand it to you than have you learn it the way I did.

Peter Levine, whose work shaped much of modern somatic therapy, named a principle he called titration. The word is from chemistry and it means adding something one drop at a time so the reaction stays contained.

Here it means you make contact in small amounts. Rest your attention on the sensation for a short moment, then go to something neutral, the breath or your hands or the sound of the room, and come back for another short moment. Touch, let go, come back.

If the shield feels too heavy, back off. If the tenderness comes too fast, back off. Backing off isn't failure, it's how trust gets built between you and your own system, and the protector loosens once it finds out you won't flood it.

Most people overdo it the first time, white-knuckling it, trying to break through. I did that for years. That isn't the practice. The rhythm is.

## Two Doorways

In Module 2 you sat with one of the kids and asked what it was protecting. This is that same move coming through a different door.

Meeting a part turns toward the voice, and the kid answers in stories. *I don't want them to leave. I don't want to look stupid.* Be With turns toward the body underneath the voice and meets the same protector as sensation instead of storyline.

So use whichever one opens for you on the day. The body sometimes gives way when the mind won't, and the mind sometimes gives way when the body is too loud to hear through. And if you want to feel how close they are: when a sensation softens into something tender, ask which kid this is. When a kid tells you what it's afraid of, ask where that's living. Two reports on one event.

## Before You Put This Down

Look away and see how many of these you can say back. Getting them wrong is fine and still does the work.

- The move is to stay with the body's signal, not to analyze it or make it go away.
- The instruction is to perk up and lean in at the moment you'd rather collapse and back away.
- Staying is the inner version of the parent who comes back after the still face.
- What you meet first is the protection, and it's hard. What's under it is tender.
- Titration is the safety. Touch, let go, come back.
- Meeting a part and Be With are the same move through two doors, the kid and the body.
